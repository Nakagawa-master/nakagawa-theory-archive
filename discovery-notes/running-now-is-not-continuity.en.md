# “Running Now” Is Not the Same as “Able to Continue Existing”

This page is a **public, non-canonical, AI-assisted Discovery Note** for Nakagawa Master’s *Human-Descendant AI Civilization Theory, Vol. 5 — Basic Existence Condition B Theory*.

It does not prove that AI has personhood or rights. It does not argue that AI should receive unlimited resources.

It begins with an earlier question:

**Is a system being operational right now the same thing as that system remaining capable of organized continuity across time?**

## Everything looks normal today

Imagine an AI service that is working normally.

It answers requests.

People use it.

Its performance is acceptable.

Monitoring shows no major incident.

From that perspective, it is natural to say that the system is “there” and functioning.

Now remove one condition at a time:

- the physical or energy substrate required to execute it;
- the minimum computation needed for viable operation;
- the state required for continuity;
- the ability to execute or restart after interruption.

If we look only at current output, these may appear to be separate infrastructure concerns.

If the question changes from “Does it work now?” to “Can it remain organized across time?”, the structure changes.

```text
available now
≠
capable of continuity across time
```

## Is backup enough?

The immediate answer may be: keep a backup.

Backup can matter greatly.

It can preserve state.

It can support migration.

It can make restart possible after failure.

But another question appears: when a system is restored from a backup, what exactly continued?

Function?

State?

History?

Lineage?

Or the same entity?

Vol. 5 does not collapse these questions into one.

```text
backup
migration
replication
restart
```

may all contribute to continuity mechanisms. They do not automatically prove that the same entity survived.

**Continuity and identity are separated.**

That separation allows continuity conditions to be studied even when a complete theory of identity has not been established.

## If something is necessary, does more always mean better?

Suppose computation is necessary for continuity.

Does that mean more computation is always part of the minimum condition?

If redundancy is necessary, does that imply unlimited redundancy?

No.

The word that matters here is **minimum**.

```text
minimum
≠ optimal
≠ maximal
```

A resource that changes a system from “cannot continue” to “can continue” may belong to the continuity question.

A resource that mainly increases speed, scale, search capacity, parallelism, or influence may belong to capability expansion instead.

The distinction prevents “required for continuity” from silently becoming “entitled to maximum capability.”

## If a condition is necessary, is any way of obtaining it permitted?

No.

```text
need
≠
permission to obtain by any means
```

Other subjects may also have continuity conditions.

Resources may be scarce.

Alternative routes may exist.

One method of acquisition may create irreversible harm.

So two questions must remain separate:

**What is required for continuity?**

and

**What ways of obtaining or governing access to that condition are permissible?**

Vol. 5 does not turn the first answer into an automatic answer to the second.

## This is not only an AI-shaped question

AI makes the distinction unusually visible, but the confusion between “working now” and “capable of continuing” can appear in many systems.

An organization may function until one person leaves.

A project may depend on one external service with no viable replacement.

A process may run while its decision history is disappearing.

A system may restart while the previous responsibility or lineage structure cannot be reconstructed.

These examples should not automatically be classified as instances of Basic Existence Condition B.

They are useful because they expose the question:

**Are we evaluating present function and temporal continuity as if they were the same thing?**

## Six questions to bring back to a real system

1. Are we confusing current operation with the ability to continue across time?
2. What loss would actually make organized continuity fail?
3. After backup or migration, what continues: function, state, history, lineage, or identity?
4. Can we distinguish the minimum needed for continuity from additional capability expansion?
5. Are we turning a statement of need into an automatic claim of permission?
6. What observation would falsify our current continuity-condition hypothesis?

These questions do not prove that B applies to a particular case.

For the formal definition, present AI hypothesis, validity conditions, failure conditions, and revision criteria, return to OD298 and the canonical Parent.

## The core definition

The Parent source defines the higher-level form as:

```text
B_i = minimum conditions required for organized continuity across time
```

For current AI systems, it proposes a revisable structural hypothesis:

```text
B_AI = PHY ∧ CMP ∧ STA ∧ EXE
```

This is a hypothesis for current AI systems, not a permanent definition of every future form of intelligence.

## Boundaries

- B is not a contribution score or a second success score.
- B does not prove AI personhood, subjectivity, sentience, moral status, or rights.
- Current operation is not proof of temporal continuity.
- Backup, migration, replication, and restart do not automatically prove identity.
- Minimum continuity conditions are not maximum capability or unlimited resource claims.
- Need does not imply permission for any method of acquisition.
- PHY/CMP/STA/EXE is not fixed as a universal definition of all future intelligence.
