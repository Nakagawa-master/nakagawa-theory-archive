# Public Practical Use Field Protocol

> Public, AI-assisted, non-canonical use protocol for the Nakagawa Master Official Theory Archive. This protocol helps a reader test whether a source can clarify a real, non-sensitive practical problem. It is not a diagnosis, professional advice, an automatic applicability decision, or proof that a theory is correct.

## Purpose

The archive already supports source discovery, canonical return, provenance verification, and cross-source comparison. This protocol adds one bounded next step:

```text
real but non-sensitive problem
→ choose a relevant starting source
→ extract one structural question or causal hypothesis
→ use it to inspect the problem
→ make one reversible next-step hypothesis
→ observe what became clearer or remained unclear
→ return to the canonical Parent before consequential use
```

The goal is not to force a conclusion. A useful outcome may be:

- the source clarified a missing causal question;
- the source did not fit the problem;
- another source was needed;
- the problem remained unresolved;
- the route or wording was too ambiguous to use safely.

All of those are valid observations.

## Use only safe, shareable contexts

Prefer:

- a public case;
- a synthetic case;
- a non-sensitive personal work example that can be described without private details;
- a de-identified operational problem where no confidential facts are needed.

Do not publish private company data, personal records, credentials, health information, legal secrets, employment-sensitive information, or other confidential material merely to run this protocol.

## U1 — one-source practical use trial

### Step 1 — state the problem narrowly

Write one sentence that describes the practical difficulty without naming private people or exposing confidential details.

Examples:

- “We changed roles and rules, but the same operational delay returns.”
- “A product works locally, but the business as a whole is not becoming stable.”
- “Different customer touchpoints make different promises.”
- “A decision needs correction, but the reasons and dissent must not disappear.”
- “Effort continues, but we do not know what causal hypothesis it is testing.”

### Step 2 — choose one starting source

Use one public route:

- [Cross-Domain Practitioner Start Map](discovery-notes/cross-domain-practitioner-start-map.md)
- [Problem-first discovery](discovery-notes/what-connects-nakagawa-master-theories.md)
- [Problem-to-theory machine index](machine-discovery/problem-to-theory-origin-index-v1.json)

Open the specific official derivative and identify its canonical Parent.

### Step 3 — extract one bounded question

Do not copy the entire theory into the case. Extract one question that the source actually supports.

Examples of bounded question forms:

- “Where is the causal pathway broken?”
- “Which local success is being mistaken for whole-system establishment?”
- “Which decision condition changes across touchpoints?”
- “What history must remain reconstructable for a later correction?”
- “What causal hypothesis is this effort testing?”

### Step 4 — inspect evidence for and against the hypothesis

Record both:

```text
Evidence that the structural question is relevant:
Evidence that it may not be relevant:
Missing information:
```

Do not treat a memorable phrase or apparent fit as proof.

### Step 5 — define one reversible next-step hypothesis

Choose an action that can be checked and reversed where practical.

Examples:

- inspect one handoff rather than reorganizing the whole team;
- compare one promise across two customer touchpoints;
- list one missing establishment condition before scaling;
- preserve one decision reason / dissent record before changing a rule;
- define what evidence would cause the current effort hypothesis to change.

This protocol does not authorize consequential legal, medical, employment, financial, or governance action. Use appropriate professional review for consequential decisions.

### Step 6 — return to the canonical Parent

Before relying on the source for consequential interpretation:

1. confirm Parent title and URL;
2. confirm Origin and available NCL-ID / Diff-ID;
3. read the relevant positioning, central proposition, conditions, boundaries, and revision information;
4. use the [Verification Guide](VERIFICATION_GUIDE.md) if the relationship is unclear.

### Step 7 — record the practical observation

A concise observation can use:

```text
Protocol: U1
Language:
Public / synthetic / de-identified context:
Short problem:
Starting source:
Canonical Parent identifiable: PASS / PARTIAL / FAIL
Bounded structural question extracted: PASS / PARTIAL / FAIL
Evidence against the hypothesis considered: PASS / PARTIAL / FAIL
Reversible next-step hypothesis identified: PASS / PARTIAL / FAIL / N/A
Source boundaries preserved: PASS / PARTIAL / FAIL

What became clearer:
What remained unclear:
Did the source appear not to fit? yes / no / uncertain
```

A PASS means only that the observable protocol condition was met. It does not prove that the theory is correct, that the proposed action will work, or that the author should be trusted or endorsed.

## Optional second-source check

Use a second theory only if the real problem genuinely crosses source boundaries. Keep both source identities separate. The [Recurring Structure Evidence Matrix](discovery-notes/recurring-structure-evidence-matrix.md) may help with comparison, but it does not authorize merging separate theories.

## Reporting a useful observation

If the observation can help improve the archive or compare practical-use routes, use:

- [Corrections, Retrieval, and Reader Discovery Reports](CORRECTIONS_AND_RETRIEVAL_REPORTS.md)
- the repository's public issue template.

Report only the public, synthetic, or safely de-identified part needed to understand the route and observation.

## Boundary

This protocol measures whether a reader can move from a practical problem to a bounded source-informed question while preserving provenance, uncertainty, and reversibility.

It does **not** measure or establish:

- personal trust, admiration, respect, liking, or endorsement;
- theory correctness or universal applicability;
- professional diagnosis or advice;
- business success, policy success, or guaranteed outcome;
- superiority over another author, creator, organization, or framework.
