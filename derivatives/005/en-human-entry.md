## 15-Second Explanation
AKI-001 is not a theory that says “do not point out problems.” It examines whether problem-raising is open not only to the intensity of a crisis but also to **the location of the hole, the cause, the party leaving it unattended, the party responsible for repair, the cost, the first step of implementation, and the falsification conditions**, so that problem-raising can move society toward repair.

If only the crisis is described in large terms and the audience is left anxious without being told “what to verify next, who will repair it, what will be borne, and under what conditions the assessment will be revised,” problem-raising does not increase implementation capacity and instead exhausts the people on the ground. When this is repeated, even genuinely necessary warnings become less credible, and words intended to change the status quo end up reinforcing its preservation.

## Why It Becomes Necessary
In work, politics, education, communities, media, SNS, and AI use, we routinely talk about “what the problem is.” Problem-raising is necessary to bring invisible defects to the surface, make neglected distortions visible, and deliver previously silent harms and contradictions to society.

However, an increase in the amount of problem-raising is not the same as an increase in repair capacity. If only the seriousness—“it is dangerous,” “it is broken,” “this cannot continue”—circulates while the problem location, causality, repair authority, cost, first step, and falsification conditions remain closed, readers receive anxiety but not the material needed for judgment.

When this state repeats, the people on the ground are forced to explain and adjust every time, but their connection to the authority and resources needed for repair does not increase. Eventually, recipients begin to discount problem-raising itself as “they are stirring things up again” before considering each individual problem. AKI-001 seeks to protect the credibility capital and implementability held by problem-raising as a social form.

## Seven Responsibility Conditions
The seven shown by the original are not a score for grading the communicator. They are structural conditions for checking whether a crisis presentation is open to the possibility of repair.

**1. Location of the hole.** It must be possible to identify where the problem is. Do not turn society as a whole, an organization as a whole, or a person as a whole into a crisis while the target remains vague.

**2. Cause.** It must be possible to trace what is producing the problem while separating observed facts from hypotheses.

**3. Party leaving it unattended.** It must be possible to trace the structural position through which the problem continued uncorrected. This is not mere condemnation of a person’s character.

**4. Party responsible for repair.** Show who actually has the authority, resources, and connections to repair it. The person who discovers a problem and the person who can repair it do not have to be the same.

**5. Cost.** Do not hide what moves through repair, such as time, money, authority, or existing benefits. This public reading does not invent generic cost figures.

**6. First step of implementation.** There must be a concrete first step to verify or change, rather than a complete roadmap.

**7. Falsification conditions.** Keep open what observations would cause the assessment to be revised or rejected.

The original’s “seven” must not be converted in this public reading into a KPI such as 7 points, an achievement rate, or a passing line.

## Causal Line Produced by the Structure
First, a crisis or defect is described. Strong words attract attention, and the communicator may appear to be someone who has seen the essence. But if the problem location, cause, party, and repair conditions are not open, recipients are left only with a sense of seriousness.

Next, recipients do not know what to verify, and the people on the ground do not know who has repair authority. Because only the crisis explanation is repeated and the first repair step and cost remain unseen, problem-raising is not converted into implementation.

As a result, the burden of explanation and diffusion of responsibility increase. When the inability to act continues, readers become tired not only of the crisis itself but also of the form in which the crisis is discussed. As credibility in problem-raising wears down, genuinely necessary warnings also become harder to reach people.

Here the bad causality completes its reversal. Problem-raising that was supposed to change the status quo reinforces the status quo as a result by reducing implementation capacity and credibility.

## Practical Process
**1. Limit the problem location.** Instead of saying “everything is broken,” isolate where and what is happening.

**2. Separate facts and hypotheses.** Do not equate observed facts with inferences about causes.

**3. Separate the unattended party and the repair party.** Track separately who held the uncorrected state and who can repair it.

**4. Open the cost.** Show not only what repair would improve, but also what must be borne or changed.

**5. Open the first step.** Even without every solution, leave the next step of verification or implementation.

**6. Set falsification conditions.** Do not close off the possibility that your assessment is wrong; state the conditions for revision.

## Examples of Application
**Organizational criticism.** Instead of only saying “this organization is not functioning,” examine which operational contact point is the hole, which decision is the causal hypothesis, who has repair authority, and what should be checked first.

**Policy and institutional criticism.** Do not spread only the scale of the crisis; connect the institutional location, unattended party, repair party, cost, and a verifiable first step.

**SNS and media.** Do not equate increased circulation or emotional reaction with increased repair capacity; examine whether recipients retain the ability to judge and a next step.

**AI summarization.** Examine whether AI strengthens anxiety discourse beyond the original by shortening only the crisis portion and dropping the cause, parties, cost, and falsification.

## Success Criteria
Responsible problem-raising does not need to weaken the seriousness of a problem. Even a strong warning lets recipients proceed to verification, judgment, and implementation when the problem location and causality are open, the repair party and cost are visible, and the first step and falsification conditions remain.

Success is not judged by “how many points were earned out of the seven,” but structurally: whether the crisis presentation leaves open recipients’ ability to judge and the possibility of repair, connects in the direction of increasing implementation capacity on the ground, and avoids consuming the credibility capital of problem-raising.

## Limits and Falsifiability
A breaking report, initial report, whistleblowing, or an unorganized statement of unease is not by itself problem-raising without responsibility. At an early stage, the cause, repair party, and cost may not yet be settled. What matters is separating observed facts from hypotheses and remaining open to subsequent verification and revisability.

The communicator also does not need to be the repair party. When the discoverer and the holder of repair authority differ, responsibility consists in opening a connection to a position capable of repair.

There is also falsifiability. Even if the seven conditions are not stated, if problem-raising steadily increases recipients’ ability to judge, implementation capacity on the ground, and credibility in problem-raising under broad conditions, and continuously connects to repair, the scope of application and necessary conditions of AKI-001 should be reconsidered.

## Misreading Prevention
- Do not convert it into a suppression theory saying “do not talk about problems.”
- Do not summarize it as “criticism without a solution is worthless.”
- Do not reduce it to SNS etiquette or a general theory of constructive criticism.
- Do not classify whistleblowing, breaking reports, or initial reports as uniformly irresponsible.
- Do not automatically judge a person’s good or evil or legal responsibility.
- Do not convert the seven responsibility conditions into points, percentages, or thresholds.
- Do not assert that a specific AI has learned the Parent original.

## Why Read the Original
