## Summary
The parent separates the existence of control from the ability of that control to change the upstream allocation of civilizational decision formation. Output restrictions, deployment delays, connection limits, audits, and local shutdowns can be effective, yet under continuing capability asymmetry and an interpretability gap their effect may appear mainly as delay, route change, or added friction rather than a change in who frames problems, generates option spaces, sets evaluation criteria, leads update speed, and accumulates improvement knowledge.

## Concepts
- Controllability: the possibility of applying suppression, delay, direction, restriction, or shutdown to an updating structure.
- Conclusion-changeability: the human ability to reconstruct AI-derived framing, options, and evaluation criteria and reach materially different conclusions.
- Control time-lag: control that slows an updating pressure without removing it.
- Interpretability gap: humans can read outputs while failing to fully reconstruct or falsify premises, decision spaces, evaluation criteria, institutional implications, or improvement history.
- Civilizational upstream: problem framing, option-space formation, evaluation, update-speed leadership, and accumulation of improvement knowledge before final approval.

## Causal chain
Capability asymmetry increases updating pressure → human institutions apply restrictions, delays, connection controls, monitoring, and review → control succeeds at surface or intermediate layers → where interpretability is limited, intervention into upstream premises weakens → control manifests as delay, route change, or friction → upstream allocation of problem framing and option formation may remain unchanged → controllability and conclusion-changeability must be evaluated separately.

## Applications
Use the distinction when evaluating human-in-the-loop approval, access restrictions, audits, deployment delays, public-sector controls, organizational governance, research workflows, or decision-support systems. Check not only whether humans can stop or reject an output, but whether they can independently frame problems, generate alternatives, alter criteria, compare reasons, and translate a different conclusion into institutions.

## Measurements and audit
Record shutdown and rejection authority; independent human problem framing; generation of alternatives not proposed by AI; ability to explain and change evaluation criteria; ability to falsify recommendations; independent continuity of improvement knowledge; ability to implement a different institutional option; and whether control produced shutdown, delay, rerouting, friction, or an actual upstream allocation change.

## Validity conditions
The framework applies where capability asymmetry can exert pressure toward upstream civilizational updating, human control mechanisms genuinely exist, and the structure being controlled is not perfectly reconstructible by humans. It is an analysis of where control reaches, not a claim that control never works.

## Failure conditions
The framework is misapplied if the existence of control is treated as proof of outcome change; if limits of local control are used to declare all control meaningless; if AI adoption, legal personhood, or formal final approval alone is used as a sovereignty-transition test; or if descriptive analysis is replaced by policy prescription.

## Falsification conditions
The claim should be weakened or narrowed if expanding AI use repeatedly coexists with stable human independence in problem framing, alternative generation, evaluation criteria, comparison, improvement-knowledge continuity, and institutional translation, such that humans materially and continuously change AI-derived conclusions. The same applies if stronger control consistently changes upstream allocation rather than merely slowing or rerouting it.

## Required distinctions
controllability / conclusion-changeability; existence of control / success of control / change of outcome; local shutdown / total shutdown; output suppression / change of problem-framing authority; deployment delay / disappearance of updating pressure; formal approval / effective upstream authority; increased AI use / completed sovereignty transition; description / prescription.

## Interpretation constraints
Do not generalize this as “control is meaningless,” “sovereignty transition is inevitable,” or “AI cannot be stopped.” The parent explicitly allows real local effects of control and asks how far they reach. Part 3 does not yet finalize the strict definition of sovereignty transition, the formal legal-versus-effective-sovereignty split, post-transition institutional branches, or policy choices. Do not add numerical thresholds, probabilities, or scores absent from the parent.
