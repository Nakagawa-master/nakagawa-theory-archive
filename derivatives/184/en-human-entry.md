## 15-Second Explanation

The Parent Original asks whether the premise “we cannot keep everyone alive unless everyone works” is really correct when viewed against modern physical productive capacity. Beginning with data such as world-average food supply exceeding the required amount, the Parent rereads scarcity not only as insufficient total quantity, but as **failure to reach people—an issue of access, distribution, and institutions**.

At the same time, modern production is nonlinear not only because of people’s current labor hours, but because of the accumulated contributions of machines, algorithms, infrastructure, software, standardization, and past knowledge. This cumulative structure creates supply surplus above the minimum survival line, and the Parent links this foundation to “civilizational basic metabolism.”

The institution is then made two-tiered. **The survival domain is protected unconditionally through LifeOS. Wealth, options, discretion, and freedom in the surplus domain open according to C through connection rewards.** This is a structural hypothesis that replaces moving people through fear of hunger with the desire for surplus—the desire to live more freely—as the driving force.

## Why It Is Needed
The objection “if survival for everyone becomes unconditional, nobody will work and supply will stop” is powerful. But it mixes at least three questions.

First, **is total supply really below the amount needed for survival?** Using food as an example, the Parent gives reference values of approximately 2,000–2,500 kcal per day as a general requirement versus approximately 2,800–3,000 kcal per person per day in world-average supply. If hunger remains, total quantity alone is not enough to explain it.

Second, **why does supply fail to reach people even when it exists?** Price, logistics, income, institutions, market structure, and distributional distortions block access to things that physically exist. Treating “shortage” as one word therefore confuses problems that require more production with problems that require better distribution.

Third, **who creates current productive capacity?** Modern factories, communications, software, medicine, and logistics are not sustained only by the labor of people who happen to be there now. Past farming technologies, mechanization, electricity, chemistry, medicine, communications, standards, and knowledge accumulate as structures and are reused in current production. The Parent reads this as a shift from the old model “time input = production” to the new model “structural input = production.”

Once these three points are separated, the issue is no longer the simple formula “making survival unconditional means taking one-sidedly from people who work now.” It becomes the question of how to design who can use, and how far they can use, the modern nonlinear production structure in which historical C has crystallized.

## Practical Process
### 1. Define the survival requirement
Clarify how to measure the minimum line for food, housing, medical care, basic infrastructure, and so on in the target region and period. The calorie values shown by the Parent are explanatory examples and should be updated with current data in operation.

### 2. Measure total supply capacity
Within the same scope as the requirement, check production, inventory, equipment capacity, and available supply margin. Here, first examine what physically exists or can be produced, rather than only what has reached people.

### 3. Separate Access / Distribution / Structure
If total quantity is sufficient but deprivation remains, look for blockages in price, logistics, region, institutions, income, eligibility, and market structure. Do not treat supply shortages and connection shortages with the same remedy.

### 4. Examine the contribution of nonlinear structure
Look not only at current labor but also at how much machines, algorithms, infrastructure, software, standards, and accumulated knowledge support production. Declining marginal cost, replicability, and accumulation are important observation axes.

### 5. Separate the survival and surplus domains
Do not make the LifeOS minimum survival level conditional on contribution points, employment, moral evaluation, or connection rewards. Put wealth, options, discretion, and freedom in a separate circuit.

### 6. Treat connection rewards as “the range of freedom to move”
The Parent’s connection reward is not merely a cash bonus. It is access to surplus production systems, options, and the range of freedom and discretion. Even if surplus access changes according to C, whether one survives does not change.

### 7. Return the free-rider debate to cost comparison
Count not only the survival consumption of people who do nothing, but also the costs of excluding them through eligibility screening, surveillance, management, and sanctions. Separately examine the E cost of monopolizing surplus and blocking connections.

### 8. Re-verify under condition Z
Use public argument, updated supporting data, and verification based on T/S/R. If surplus above the required amount does not actually exist, or if surplus allocation produces phenomenon M—structural malfunction—revise the hypothesis.

## Examples of Application
### A “Food Shortage” Meeting
When a meeting says “there is not enough food,” do not start with production-increase measures. Break down total supply, required amount, waste, logistics, prices, income, and regional differences. If total quantity is insufficient, use Supply measures; if food does not reach people, separate it into Access / Distribution measures.

### Value Distribution from Automation
When an automated line or software can reproduce the same value for many people, do not explain its productive power only through current person-hours. Read it with the accumulation of historical C in equipment, knowledge, standards, past development, public infrastructure, and so on.

### Minimum Guarantee Systems
When designing a minimum guarantee, do not begin with “how should we punish people who do nothing?” First check the supply margin and access routes that physically support the minimum survival line. Treat the range of surplus use as a separate connection design.

### Organizational Reward Design
Instead of motivating people only through fear of losing their livelihood, design the surplus domain around greater discretion, interesting work, options, access to resources, and so on. However, do not directly convert the Parent’s concepts into personnel scores; specify the definition of C and its verifiability separately.

### Auditing Exclusion Systems
If a system excludes free riders through eligibility screening, surveillance, and sanctions, compare its operating cost with the additional consumption of making survival unconditional. The Parent presents a structure in which exclusion can cost more, but evidence for each concrete system is needed individually.

## Success Criteria
Success for this structure is not determined by one result such as “everyone has the same life,” “nobody works,” or “government spending increases/decreases.”

At minimum, look for these directions to hold simultaneously:

- Physical supply capacity sufficient for the survival requirement is maintained.
- Connection barriers that prevent available total supply from reaching people are reduced.
- The survival domain is not made conditional on C or connection rewards.
- The contributions of nonlinear production and historical C are made visible.
- Access to the surplus domain remains mobile rather than fixed by status.
- Even as survival anxiety weakens, voluntary C such as research, creation, enterprise, and joint activity is maintained or increases.
- E in the form of surplus monopolization and connection blocking is visible and adjustable.
- Supporting data and institutional results are disclosed, and the hypothesis can be updated.

## Limitations
The Parent’s strong claims rely on modern civilization’s physical supply capacity and nonlinear production. However, a world average being sufficient does not automatically mean that logistics, medical care, energy, and housing are sufficient in every region at the same time. Local and time-limited constraints such as war, disasters, geography, deteriorating equipment, broken supply chains, and shortages of medical personnel must be observed separately.

Nonlinear production also does not mean “human work becomes zero.” The more productive the structure, the more important conditions such as maintenance, renewal, safety, resources, and energy become. The Parent does not regard current labor as the only source of productive value; it does not regard current practical work as worthless.

Nor does the Parent fix a concrete scoring algorithm, national system, tax rate, or rationing rate for connection rewards. Do not invent formulas on the derivative side.

## Preventing Misreading
- “There is surplus” ≠ “all resources are infinite.”
- “Survival is unconditional” ≠ “all wealth is distributed equally and unconditionally.”
- “Nonlinear production” ≠ “labor, maintenance, and renewal are unnecessary.”
- “Historical C supports it” ≠ “people working now support nobody.”
- “Connection reward” ≠ “a credit score for taking away survival.”
- “Surplus access according to C” ≠ “a hierarchy of human dignity.”
- “Questioning the rationality of excluding free riders” ≠ “safety measures or legal responsibility are unnecessary.”
- “World-average supply exceeds the required amount” ≠ “deprivation has ended in every region.”
- The Parent’s reference values depend on time and must be updated according to condition Z.
