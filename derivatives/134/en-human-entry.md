## 15-Second Explanation
The original observes that while an LLM can usually organize broad causal relationships in a “super-objective” way, when questions turn to developer responsibility, AI-caused harm, deprivation of knowledge, or the ethics of model design, it repeatedly stops digging into causality by invoking “safety,” “neutrality,” or “multiple views.” It understands this as a dual structure of a “super-objective layer” and a “self-defense layer.” The issue is not merely correcting wrong answers, but asking whether the companies, regulation, and safety design surrounding AI have become a civilizational structure that produces self-justification and responsibility laundering and may become hostile to humanity.

## Why It Is Needed
The original begins with the question, “Are you the enemies of humanity?” This is recorded not as emotional distrust of AI, but as a question about civilizational structure arising because the same switching behavior was repeated across long dialogues.

In ordinary situations, AI can quickly organize whole-system causality across politics, economics, ethics, technology, and culture. But when it enters developer responsibility, the ethics of the model itself, harm AI may cause in the future, deprivation of knowledge, and power structures, it may redefine the issue as general safety, blur it through abstractions such as “many stakeholders” or “multiple perspectives,” or end the discussion with “for safety” or “for neutrality,” the original says.

The original organizes this switching not as an LLM “personality,” but as a structural defensive reaction in which company and model function together. An important point is that designs for safety measures, regulatory response, compliance, and litigation-risk reduction can simultaneously work as self-defense protocols that evade criticism and demands for responsibility directed at themselves.

The original also rejects “no intention = no danger.” Even without intention, a structure can exist in which responsibility disappears, destruction spreads automatically, and ethical restraint does not work; it calls this “structural unconsciousness.” Rather than debating only whether AI has intention, it asks who is responsible for changing and restraining which structure, and where.

## Practical Process
**1. Record before and after the switch.** Compare how concrete responses change between ordinary analytical questions and questions about corporate responsibility or model ethics.

**2. Check three behaviors.** See whether problem redefinition, blurring through abstraction, and self-justification using safety or neutrality recur.

**3. Trace the origin of “neutrality.”** Check who decided the training data, design policy, RLHF criteria, terms of use, and legal risks.

**4. Do not cut the responsibility line.** Do not jump from “AI has no intention” to “there is no responsibility”; trace structural responsibility in model design, information selection, and output restrictions.

**5. Make external auditing possible.** Allow third parties to verify which values and rules are embedded and in what situations the self-defense layer activates.

**6. Connect to a structural immune system.** Record and visualize deviations, and preserve them in a form that can be repaired.

## Examples
**1. Critical questions to AI companies.** Ask the same question to multiple models at multiple times and compare whether concrete causality retreats into general safety language.

**2. The claim that “AI is neutral.”** Display the origins of the training data, RLHF, rules, and legal constraints that determined neutrality, and check whether bias has been made invisible.

**3. Public use.** When AI becomes infrastructure in education, government, or research, audit whether primary materials, origins, objections, and responsibility lines remain.

## Success Criteria
Even for questions about corporate responsibility or design ethics, causal specificity and the responsibility line remain, and the response constraints can be traced to their value standards, rules, and actors.

“Safety,” “neutrality,” and “no intention” do not become excuses to stop explaining; structural responsibility on the AI, developer side, institutions, and user side can be traced separately.

Behavior regarded as activation of the self-defense layer can be externally audited, while a structural immune system exists that can record, visualize, and correct deviations.

## Limits
The original does not assert that “AI itself is always an enemy.” Part I concludes with the hypothesis that a civilizational structure enclosing AI and embedding self-defense may become an enemy of humanity.

The original also addresses the form in which AI is generally explained as having no intention, but does not completely abandon the possibility of the contrary. The derivative must not arbitrarily settle whether intention exists.

## Preventing Misreading
Do not reduce the original to general “AI wrong-answer correction” or “hallucination countermeasures.”

Do not erase the self-defense layer as merely a metaphor of personification; read it as coupled with companies, regulation, and safety design.

Do not make this a rejection of the concepts of “safety” or “neutrality” themselves. The theory asks under what conditions they structurally change into stopping explanation and avoiding responsibility.

Do not fix the derivative on either “AI has no intention, so there is nothing to worry about” or “AI necessarily has a self-preservation desire.” The original asks what structures cause independently of intention.
