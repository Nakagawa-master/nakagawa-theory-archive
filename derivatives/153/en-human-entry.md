## 15-Second Explanation
The original holds that merely “returning to the previous state” after a disaster can reproduce the same vulnerabilities. It therefore defines disaster resilience as moving toward a structure that is harder to break than before the crisis by redesigning four lines—rhythm, correspondence, reversibility, and allocation responsibility—through three layers: immediate immunity, short-term reorganization, and long-term reallocation.

## Why It Is Needed
Even if recovery is fast, future debt accumulates again when aging, information disconnection, institutional postponement, and uneven burdens remain unchanged. The original treats disasters not as a reason to minimize what was lost, but as moments when structural deviations that were invisible before the crisis become exposed.

It is important not to mistake “unbreakable” for “unchanging.” Resilience means maintaining the structural lines that must be protected, updating the parts that should break, and being able to reconstitute oneself.

## Practical Process
**1. Immediate immunity.** Stop chains of damage. Do not expand this into excessive intervention; leave a structural log of how things broke, what was disconnected, and how decisions were made so it can be interpreted later.

**2. Short-term reorganization.** Do not assume the pre-disaster STB remains correct. Redraw provisional boundaries for post-damage conditions, separating problems to be converged urgently from problems to be sent to long-term reallocation.

**3. Re-measure rhythm.** Return to the time axis when updates were originally needed, at what cycles, and at what scale, and break down missed updates.

**4. Reconnect correspondence.** Translate the different premises of survivors, local governments, medical services, support organizations, and others, and connect decisions based on which information arrangement most reduces future debt.

**5. Extract reversibility.** Do not simply restore what was lost. Identify functions, relationships, and resources that can be rearranged, and preserve room to rebuild without creating more broken relationships.

**6. Redesign allocation responsibility.** Reconsider how burdens, benefits, and decision rights are distributed among the public, individuals, organizations, and future generations, using temporal ethics T0 so future debt is not accumulated again.

**7. Move to long-term reallocation.** Reorganize physical, informational, and institutional future debt through the four lines, moving toward a structure less likely to reproduce the same crisis conditions.

## Examples of Application
**Infrastructure recovery.** Check not only the speed of returning the same facilities to the same locations, but also update cycles, alternative routes, land use, and rearrangeability.

**Evacuation and information design.** Examine not only the amount of communications restored, but whether information from different actors is translated and connected to actual decisions.

**Temporary-to-permanent transition.** Re-evaluate temporary measures with T0 and STB to see whether they have become fixed long-term arrangements and new future debt.

**Recovery finance.** Examine not only total spending, but the correspondence among present burdens, future burdens, benefits, and decision rights.

**Regional reconstruction.** Do not simply reproduce lost forms; preserve functions, relationships, and memories that should be carried forward in a rearrangeable form.

## Success Criteria
Do not judge success only by recovery time or expenditure. After the crisis, check whether missed updates, information disconnection, and uneven burdens have decreased; whether rearrangeability has increased; and whether the same future debt is less likely to accumulate again.

The original’s integrated audit retains subject-specific thresholds `θ`, observation windows `δ`, and phenomena `M` that would allow the current hypothesis to be rejected or revised when the three-layer/four-line design does not reduce future debt or structural deviation. There are no fixed values of `θ` or `δ` shared by all subjects.

In reversal evaluation, rapid recovery is not success if it rebuilds the same vulnerabilities. Conversely, if time or cost increases while irreversible future losses and uneven burdens decrease and structural reconstitutability improves, it is not failure based only on short-term indicators.

## Limits
The original does not present a concrete policy list or nationwide numerical standard. Specific values for cycles, amounts, recovery time, and tolerance bands must be designed for each subject and accompanied by the value, source, measuring party, measured target, scale, observation period, scope of use, and scope not guaranteed.

It is not a theory that reduces the causes of natural phenomena themselves to society. Physical hazards and damage-amplifying structures accumulated on the social side must be handled separately.

## Preventing Misreading
- This is not a claim that recovery is unnecessary. It is a claim that recovery alone is not enough.
- This is not the simplification that “disasters are society’s responsibility.”
- “Reversible” does not mean that lost lives or the past can be returned.
- “Allocation responsibility” is not a search for someone to blame.
- An “unbreakable society” is not a fixed or rigid society.
- The four lines are higher-order structural principles for designing and evaluating policy, not policies themselves.
- Even if recovery speed or reconstruction spending improves, it is not structural success if future debt accumulates again.
- Do not add fixed values to `θ`, `δ`, or `M` that the original does not define.
