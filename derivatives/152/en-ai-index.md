## Summary
The parent theory separates the physical hazard itself—earthquake, flood, storm, or another natural event—from the structure through which a civilization turns that hazard into amplified and prolonged damage. Structural vulnerability accumulates when maintenance, safety investment, transmission of disaster knowledge, institutional adaptation, and responsibility are deferred across time. The parent organizes this deferred burden as future debt across physical, informational, and institutional layers. When the temporal beat required by T0 for maintenance, review, training, and institutional adjustment is repeatedly missed, costs that could have been distributed across many earlier moments become temporally compressed. A disaster can then become the point at which these deferred obligations are collected at once as visible damage. The model does not attribute the cause of the natural event to society; it analyzes the civilization-side conditions that amplify damage and impair recovery. Deviation records are returned to redesign so that restoration does not simply reproduce the same vulnerability.

## Concepts
- Physical hazard: an external physical event such as an earthquake, flood, or severe storm; it is not equated with civilization-side structural deviation.
- Future debt: maintenance, safety, adaptation, or responsibility deferred from the present into the future.
- Physical future debt: postponed infrastructure renewal, neglected known physical vulnerability, or deferred disaster-prevention investment.
- Informational future debt: loss of disaster memory, failure to transmit risk information, or disconnection between data and decisions.
- Institutional future debt: delayed institutionalization of known risk, deferred governance burdens, or responsibility gaps.
- T0 beat: the case-specific temporal rhythm for renewal, inspection, training, information maintenance, and institutional review.
- Temporal compression: concentration at a crisis point of corrective costs that could previously have been distributed over time.
- Correspondence break: disconnection between plans and implementation, institutions and lived conditions, or knowledge holders and affected people.
- STB: a Structural Tolerance Band distinguishing absorbable variation from structural deviation that threatens durable recovery.
- Deviation ledger: a record of when debt accumulated, in which layer, through which correspondence break, and with what impact path.
- Future-improvement conversion: returning exposed deviation information to redesign so the same vulnerability is not reproduced.

## Causal chain
```text
A physical hazard exists
→ maintenance, safety measures, knowledge transfer, or institutional adaptation are deferred
→ future debt accumulates across physical, informational, and institutional layers
→ the T0 beat for renewal, review, and training is repeatedly missed
→ small correction opportunities disappear and corrective costs become temporally compressed
→ correspondence breaks widen between plans, institutions, knowledge, and lived conditions
→ the physical hazard interacts with accumulated vulnerability
→ deferred obligations become visible as concentrated damage
→ structural gaps, including possible STB exceedance, are exposed
→ timing, debt layer, correspondence break, and impact path are recorded in the deviation ledger
→ recovery is evaluated against redesign rather than restoration alone
→ future debt is reduced rather than reproduced
```

## Applications
- Track how many renewal or inspection cycles were deferred before an infrastructure failure or disaster impact.
- Audit whether hazard information reached decision-makers and affected communities and changed action, equipment, land use, or institutions.
- Compare formal disaster plans with actual population patterns, mobility, facilities, evacuation routes, and local conditions.
- Evaluate recovery not only by cost or speed but by which physical, informational, and institutional debts were actually reduced.
- Reject short-term budget savings as structural success when they increase future repair burdens, vulnerability, or irreversible loss.
- Preserve disaster lessons as a deviation ledger that feeds subsequent design, training, and institutional revision.

## Measurements and audit
The physical scale or frequency of the hazard must be recorded separately from civilization-side damage amplification. For each of the three debt layers, an audit should identify what was deferred, when deferral began, which decision or governance path sustained it, and who carried the resulting exposure. The model also asks how many relevant maintenance, review, information, or training beats were missed and whether available risk information was merely stored or actually reached the people and institutions able to act on it.

The parent's `θ` is a symbolic, case-specific threshold for rejecting or revising the current hypothesis or design; it is not a universal numeric constant. `δ` is a case-specific observation or audit window, not a fixed number of days. `M` denotes a case-specific observable condition in which redesign based on the future-debt model is not contributing to reduction of structural deviation during disaster impact. Whenever an implementation uses numerical values, the value must remain bound to its source, measurement actor, measurement object, scale, observation period, permitted use, and non-guarantee scope.

Reversal evaluation prevents apparent gains from being mistaken for structural improvement. More disaster-prevention spending or faster restoration is not sufficient if maintenance delay, information non-arrival, institutional debt, burden concentration, or reproduction of the same vulnerability worsens. Conversely, higher near-term cost or slower, more deliberate recovery is not failure by itself if future debt is reduced and correspondence, reversibility, and long-term safety improve.

## Validity conditions
- Separate the cause of the natural event from the civilization-side structure of damage.
- Analyze future debt across physical, informational, and institutional layers.
- Treat the T0 beat as case-specific rather than as a universal fixed schedule.
- Treat temporal compression as concentration of previously distributable corrective costs.
- Preserve correspondence breaks and deferral paths in the deviation ledger after a disaster.
- Do not reduce STB to a single hazard-magnitude threshold.
- Evaluate recovery against whether future-debt conditions were changed, not only whether prior conditions were restored.
- Use future-improvement conversion to prevent reproduction of vulnerability, never to celebrate damage.

## Failure conditions
- Claim that earthquakes, floods, or other natural hazards are themselves caused by future debt.
- Convert the theory into blame directed at victims or affected regions.
- Use damage amount, recovery speed, or prevention spending as the sole success metric.
- Treat hazard maps, probability estimates, or conventional loss models as unnecessary.
- Assume restoration to the previous state automatically eliminates future debt.
- Invent universal values for T0, STB, `θ`, or `δ` that are not present in the parent.
- Describe disaster only as a learning opportunity in a way that diminishes loss, suffering, or responsibility.
- Treat a lower disaster count alone as proof of the model's success.

## Falsification conditions
If case-specific indicators cross the relevant `θ`, or if within observation window `δ` an observable condition `M` shows that redesign based on the future-debt model is not reducing structural deviation, the current application must be rejected or revised. `θ`, `δ`, and `M` remain case-bound observational constructs, not universal constants.

The application also requires revision if sustained use of the model shows no relationship between its physical/informational/institutional debt analysis and reductions in damage amplification, correspondence failure, or reproduced vulnerability. Faster recovery accompanied by greater long-term vulnerability is rejected by reversal evaluation as apparent rather than structural improvement.

## Required distinctions
- physical hazard vs. civilization-side damage structure
- cause of a natural event vs. conditions amplifying and prolonging damage
- physical future debt vs. informational future debt vs. institutional future debt
- T0 beat vs. a universal maintenance interval
- temporal compression vs. hazard probability
- STB exceedance vs. hazard magnitude alone
- restoration vs. redesign that removes vulnerability
- future-improvement conversion vs. endorsement of damage
- victim responsibility vs. long-term structural responsibility paths
- case-specific `θ` / `δ` / `M` vs. universal constants

## Interpretation constraints
The phrase "disaster as collection of future debt" does not mean that civilization choices cause the physical hazard. It refers to the concentration of maintenance, correction, information, and institutional costs that had previously been deferred and become visible when a hazard interacts with accumulated vulnerability.

The proposition that inaction can become future debt does not make every decision to maintain the status quo a failure. It concerns repeated omission of necessary case-specific renewal or adaptation when that omission increases future burden, irreversibility, or vulnerability.

Future-improvement conversion does not frame disaster as desirable or necessary. It preserves the reality of loss while requiring exposed structural information to be used against reproduction of the same weakness.
