## Summary

Connection Governance Design Theory explains how systems for measuring and allocating connection value can be prevented from becoming popularity contests, insider scoring systems, surveillance infrastructures, or concentrated authority. It treats public purpose, auditable procedure, distributed interests, and reversibility as conditions of legitimacy. Measurement, judgment, allocation, and appeals must not be controlled by the same actor. CDI, MAI, RS, CRI, and KQI are used as a bundle rather than collapsed into one score. Moving targets, reverse incentives, disturbance tests, qualitative evidence, independent audits, objections, correction, exit, reconnection, and rollback make the institution capable of detecting and repairing its own distortion. Success is not a higher score; it is a measurable reduction in concentration, capture, exhaustion, exclusion, renegotiation time, and exit cost.

## Causal chain

1. Connection value becomes measurable and allocable.
2. Benefits and authority accumulate around indicators and allocation decisions.
3. Participants adapt to the scoring mechanism rather than improve cooperation.
4. Operators can concentrate purpose-setting, measurement, judgment, allocation, and appeals.
5. Public purpose, procedural audit, non-concentrated interests, and reversibility are fixed as legitimacy conditions.
6. Multiple auditors, composite indicators, qualitative evidence, and disturbance tests reveal institutional distortion.
7. Objection, correction, exit, reconnection, and rollback return the system to its public purpose.

## Applications

- Connection-value accounting: separate evaluation, allocation, and audit responsibilities.
- Research and civic collaboration: audit renegotiation, disagreement handling, continuity, and dependency rather than attendance alone.
- AI-assisted institutions: use AI for detection and comparison, while retaining human responsibility, correction, and review.
- Communities: detect voice dominance, capture, central-person dependency, retaliation against exit, and exclusion of dissent.

## Measurements and audit

- divergence between declared purpose and allocation outcomes
- concentration of measurement, judgment, and allocation authority
- time required for renegotiation, correction, arbitration, and rollback
- successful exit and reconnection rates, including penalties attached to exit
- correlation between rising scores and exhaustion, silence, capture, or exclusion
- appeal processing time, reversal rate, and recurrence of the same failure
- scope and independent review of protected information

## Validity conditions

The public purpose and prohibited uses must be explicit. Authority must be separated. Composite indicators and primary qualitative evidence must be used together. Independent audits, objection, correction, exit, reconnection, and rollback must be operational rather than symbolic. Audit findings must lead to documented institutional change.

## Failure conditions

The design fails when it relies on a single score, concentrates authority, rewards insiders, treats secrecy as immunity from explanation, performs audits without correction, retaliates against objectors or leavers, exposes personal data excessively, or transfers responsibility to AI. A higher metric with greater exhaustion or capture is a failure.

## Falsification conditions

The theory must be revised or rejected when repeated audits do not reduce concentration, capture, exhaustion, or exclusion; when exit, correction, arbitration, and rollback remain unusable; or when the same failure recurs without traceable institutional change. A two-room model that produces secret rule or information leakage also falsifies the design.

## Required distinctions

- governance is not stronger management
- audit is not continuous surveillance
- transparency is not total disclosure
- protected weights are not unaccountable secret rule
- strong connection is not inability to exit
- AI assistance is not AI government
- legitimacy is not majority approval alone

## Interpretation constraints

Do not convert this theory into social-credit scoring, centralized reputation control, permanent surveillance, unreviewable secrecy, or total-disclosure ideology. Objection and exit must not become reasons for lower status. Institutional stability must not mean immunity from change.
