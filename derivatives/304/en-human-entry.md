## 15-Second Explanation

An actor with more computing resources may show higher ability on a task and then receive more resources next time. Those resources create the next ability, which gathers still more resources. Theory 11 does not treat ability differences themselves as evil; it asks when this cycle continues, makes others' attempts increasingly difficult, and turns past advantage into a permanent entitlement.

## Why It Is Needed

### Auditing ability also requires resources

Theory 10 said that it is not enough merely to appoint a separate auditor: evidence, authority, failure causes, interests, and objection routes must be separated. But even a separate auditor can become merely nominally independent if it cannot reach the materials, lacks computing resources for analysis, or lacks the power to formulate an objection. Theory 11 examines how resources supporting ability and auditing are distributed, and how that distribution creates the next ability gap.

### “Can do it now” becomes “gets priority next time”

An actor uses substantial compute and produces a good result. Allocating more resources next time after seeing that result may be reasonable temporarily. The problem is when that allocation itself develops the next ability and repeats as “it performed well this time, so send it here again.” The side that did not receive resources loses the chance to build a record, and “no record” may become the reason for the next non-allocation.

### A difference in size alone is not a class

Even a large ability gap is not fixed if allocation can be revised when conditions change and another actor can participate through a new method. Conversely, even a small gap needs attention if it keeps creating the next resource gap and later becomes irreversible. Theory 11 looks less at a single inequality table than at the mechanism by which a difference recreates itself across time.

## Practical Process

1. **Identify what ability means.** Do not equate computing resources with ability. Separate the task, information, time, tools, and environment, and examine what additional resources actually changed.
2. **Examine the link between evaluation and allocation.** Ask what the actual causal contribution was, rather than output volume or speed. Record who allocated the next resources, and for what reason.
3. **Follow it to the next point in time.** Check whether this allocation returns as next-period ability, record, or influence. Separate a one-off advantage from recurring positive feedback.
4. **Separate necessity.** Distinguish minimum condition B, which must continue for the organization to exist, from growth demand that expands ability further. B can be reviewed, but do not treat every increased demand as B.
5. **Separate authority.** Even when high ability is used as evidence, do not automatically grant governance rights or audit exemption. Examine influence over the resource-allocation rules themselves.
6. **Confirm the way back.** Check whether reassessment after conditions change, changed allocation, alternative access, and re-entry for delayed actors are genuinely possible.

## Examples

### Concentrating resources on a high-performing actor

Under limited time or high failure costs, temporarily concentrating on an actor with a record may be justified. But when the emergency ends, do not continue solely because “it is strong now because we concentrated before.” The dividing line is whether it can be reconsidered against the current purpose and contribution.

### An alternative has no record

The alternative may lack a record because it lacks ability. But it may also be because it received neither resources nor opportunities to try. Separate these cases and leave an entry point—re-entry—from which ability can be shown through another method or role. This does not guarantee the same result.

### Adopting a high-ability judgment

Using high ability in a specific field is different from giving that actor permanent decision authority in every field. Even a high-performing actor may misread the same information sources or self-audit allocations that affect it.

## Success Criteria

Even when allocating more resources, the reason can be restated from the current task, contribution, and necessity. The causal link between advantage and next-period allocation can be traced, and the allocation can be reconsidered when conditions change. Another actor has room to return to evaluation and ability-building through a different resource path or new method, and auditors can perform independent analysis and evidence access. This does not mean equalizing everyone's ability or resources.

## Limits

Compute does not necessarily increase ability proportionally, nor does high ability necessarily affect the next allocation. The cycle in Theory 11 is a structural hypothesis whose arrows must be observed and falsified. Do not establish a class from resource concentration, ability differences, or continued advantage alone; examine the reason for persistence, reversibility, and re-entry together. Reassessment and dispersal are not万能 either: revise their conditions if they damage ability formation.

## Misreading Prevention

High ability does not prove existence value, moral status, entitlement to govern, or permanent priority for resources. Minimum continuation condition B is not a guarantee of maximum ability. Rewards for contribution may exist, but past contribution must not become permanent status. This does not claim that current AI has a personal desire to rule or class consciousness. How to count “one actor” across copied or forked intelligence remains for Theory 12. For detailed grounds and reservations, return to the original at the Parent URL.
