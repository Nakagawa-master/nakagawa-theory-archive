## Summary

Volume 10 treated independent audit as a causal separation of evidence, authority, failure domains, interests, and objection paths. Volume 11 asks what happens when the ability to use those paths is itself unequal. An auditor may be formally distinct yet unable to obtain evidence, perform sustained analysis, or formulate an effective objection without information access, time, computation, and continuity resources.

The central structural hypothesis is a conditional feedback loop: `compute advantage → task-specific capability advantage → evaluation / selection → allocation / influence advantage → resource advantage → next compute / capability advantage`. Its concern is not a difference at one instant. It is the possibility that a current advantage becomes a condition producing the next advantage, persists across time, and reduces reversibility and re-entry for other subjects. The arrows are not automatic laws. Each must be observed, and countervailing arrows can weaken or break the loop.

This derivative does not advocate identical compute or resources for all, prohibit high capability, or treat every concentration as class formation. Temporary concentration can be warranted by scarcity, efficiency, urgency, or high failure costs. The proposed boundary is to prevent a temporary role or allocation from becoming an unreviewable permanent entitlement. A valid current reason can justify continued allocation; a past advantage alone cannot settle all future allocations. The article separates present observations, a structural hypothesis, a future-condition hypothesis concerning possible AI successor civilizations, and a normative proposal. It does not establish present AI personhood, subjective desires, political sovereignty, or an already-existing AI civilization class system.

## Concepts

- **Compute:** Available computational conditions for processing, inference, search, judgment, or execution, not a specific hardware unit or an intrinsic rank of a subject. More compute may change what is feasible, but it does not guarantee proportional capability gains.
- **Capability:** A task- and condition-dependent ability shaped by information, memory, algorithms, tools, execution setting, and time as well as compute. Speed, accuracy, breadth, long-horizon handling, and action are distinct slices, not one universal superiority scale.
- **Resource:** A wider set of capability-forming conditions that can include compute, information access, time, continuity, executable environments, verification, tools, and physical infrastructure. The article does not prescribe a detailed allocation scheme for these items.
- **Contribution / S=C:** Actual causal contribution to an outcome, distinct from potential capability and output volume. Reward for contribution can be justified without converting a past contribution into permanent priority.
- **B (baseline existence conditions):** Minimum conditions for organized continuity into a subsequent time, independent of contribution. B is revisable when circumstances change but is neither maximum capability nor an unconditional right to all resources required for growth.
- **Governance sovereignty:** Legitimate governing authority, not the same as factual influence, task-specific performance, prediction quality, compute holdings, or a conditional operational role.
- **Self-reinforcement:** A current difference changes the conditions that produce the next difference. It needs a return edge from allocation and resource access to later compute or capability, not merely an initial advantage.
- **Durable class-formation signal:** Self-reinforcement plus persistence, reduced reversibility, and reduced re-entry. This is a causal observation framework, not a numeric threshold, automatic moral verdict, or proof from one snapshot.
- **Reversibility:** The ability to reassess roles, evaluations, access, and allocations as conditions change. It does not require destroying every advantage or withdrawing resources arbitrarily and instantly.
- **Re-entry:** The possibility that a disadvantaged subject can again form capabilities and be evaluated through new methods, roles, or access paths. It is not a promise of equal results, equal capability, or identical resource shares.

## Causal chain

### Amplification and lock-in

An initial difference in compute can, under relevant task conditions, affect capability. Capability can influence an evaluation or selection decision; that decision can affect resource access and institutional influence. Resources received today become inputs to tomorrow's learning, search, execution, and audit capacity. If the new capability again influences evaluation and allocation, the path returns to its own starting conditions. The explanatory path is therefore longer than the shorthand `compute → capability → resource → compute`: it includes evaluation, selection, allocation, and influence as inspectable mediators.

Evaluation error can travel through the same path. A mistaken early assessment may produce a resource gap; that gap changes later capabilities and observable performance; the later performance can then appear to validate the original assessment. To distinguish an innate or pre-existing capability difference from one created by previous allocation, preserve the timing and reasons for each decision. Output that is easy to count can be overvalued even when it is not the highest causal contribution.

The less-resourced side can face a complementary loop: limited access → fewer chances to form capability and a record of performance → less visible evidence of capability → weaker evaluation → still less access. Absence of a track record is not proof of inability if the opportunity to build that record was missing. On the advantaged side, previous concentration can create dependency and switching costs; those effects can themselves become reasons for further concentration. Scarcity, uncertainty, urgency, efficiency pressure, scale effects, and high failure costs may strengthen these paths without being inherently wrongful.

### Reassessment and damping

Current advantage → reassessment of current task capability, actual contribution, resource need, B versus growth, impacts on others, and alternative paths → separation of temporary role from permanent status → allocation that can be revised as conditions change → alternative access → renewed capability formation and evaluation → re-entry and damping of the feedback. A genuine present need or contribution can still support a continued advantage. The point is to distinguish that current justification from automatic renewal based solely on prior allocation or a status inherited from past success.

The loop can also weaken without intervention: additional compute may have diminishing effects, high capability may become costly to maintain, or a new method may produce comparable capability with fewer resources. The hypothesis must make room for these countervailing edges. Neither decentralization nor repeated reassessment is automatically correct if it destroys necessary capability, coordination, or timely action.

## State model

| State | Relevant variables | Question for the next transition |
|---|---|---|
| Initial access | Compute, information, time, tools, infrastructure | Which conditions are relevant to the task? |
| Capability formation | Task performance, methods, marginal returns | Did additional access actually change the capability? |
| Evaluation / selection | Criteria, observability, contribution, uncertainty | How was capability interpreted and who selected the next recipient? |
| Allocation / influence | Resources, roles, access, rule-setting influence | Does this output of evaluation become the next period's input? |
| Persistence | Prior success, dependence, switching costs | Is continuation justified by present conditions or inherited privilege? |
| Lock-in signal | Repeated feedback, less reversibility, less re-entry | Can a different subject still form and demonstrate capability? |
| Damping | Reassessment, non-permanent status, alternative access | Does correction remain feasible without destroying useful capacity? |

The unit of analysis is an edge across time, not a fixed ranking of subjects. At time t, an allocation may shape capability at t+1; the resulting performance may shape allocation at t+2. A single cross-sectional comparison cannot establish this causal return. A large gap may coexist with high mobility and reversibility, while a smaller gap may be structurally consequential if it persistently reproduces itself.

## Applications

### Concentrating compute in a high-performing system

For a specific urgent task or high failure cost, allocating more resources to a presently capable system may be reasonable. Test whether extra compute improves the relevant capability, how the result relates to actual causal contribution, and whether the allocation rationale remains valid as conditions change. Track whether the concentration creates dependency and suppresses alternative capability formation. Concentration alone is not proof of a class; concentration justified mainly by the previous concentration, persisting after its initial reason disappears, is a feedback signal to audit.

### Evaluating a new or previously excluded subject

An established performer has a useful track record. A newer alternative may lack one because it had no comparable access, not because it cannot perform. Maintain the distinction between evidence of inability and absence of evidence caused by lack of opportunity. A re-entry path can involve a different method, role, information source, or resource route; it does not have to promise equivalent performance or the same allocation. The comparison should include the cost of learning about alternatives and the long-run cost of never learning.

### Treating growth as a protected baseline need

Separate the minimum resource conditions for organized continuity from additional demand to expand capability. A more complex system can genuinely change some continuity requirements, so B is revisable. But `B is revisable` does not mean every new expense created by expansion becomes a protected minimum. Otherwise expansion → larger demand → demand relabeled as B → priority allocation → further expansion becomes a second reinforcement loop. Need(B) does not authorize unlimited acquisition or negate the baseline conditions and future options of others.

### Using superior analysis without granting sovereignty

A highly capable subject may offer better analysis and be assigned a significant conditional role. Factual influence is not legitimate permanent governance authority. Task-specific performance does not settle unrelated domains, incentives, evidence independence, failure domains, or objection paths. If higher capability becomes an exemption from independent audit or control over the allocation rules that feed its own capability, the governance edge intensifies the loop. Compute ownership also cannot itself confer a governing right.

### Emergency allocation and auditor resources

Under urgency, using an existing capable system can be rational. The emergency is not a permanent resource privilege; after pressure falls, the allocation reason must be open to reassessment, without imposing a mechanical expiry period. Likewise, multiple named auditors do not guarantee effective plurality if only one actor can access independent evidence, pay the analytical cost, or sustain an objection. The response is to examine actual correction capacity, not demand identical resource quantities for every auditor.

### Scale, switching, and dependency

Large infrastructure can have real economies of scale. A high switching cost can also preserve a previously rational choice. Neither effect alone proves illegitimate class formation. Trace whether current performance still explains the allocation, whether alternatives are made untestable by previous concentration, and whether dependency becomes an automatic justification for further concentration. Test the counterfactual possibility of a different route without assuming costless redistribution.

## Measurements and audit

1. Does the compute difference reliably affect the relevant task capability, or are other conditions decisive?
2. Does the capability difference influence the next allocation through an identifiable evaluation and selection process?
3. Does privileged resource access change future compute or capability, rather than merely accompany it?
4. Does resource priority persist after capability or contextual conditions decline? If so, what currently explains it?
5. Can a previously disadvantaged subject return to capability formation and evaluation through another route?
6. Does a temporary allocation survive as permanent status after the original need has gone?
7. Is factual capability being converted into governance rights, rule-setting influence, or audit exemption?
8. Is demand created by expansion repeatedly relabeled as B, the minimum continuity condition?
9. Is emergency concentration reassessed when emergency pressure subsides?
10. Are evidence access, analytical computation, and objection-forming capacity for independent audit concentrated despite formal auditor plurality?
11. Does concentration itself become the primary reason for its next renewal?

These observations are not a weighted score or proof of an injustice. Correlation may reflect a third variable, selection effects, or reverse causation. Reconstruct each conditional edge over time and compare plausible counter-explanations. The article fixes no universal threshold θ, allocation quota, or review interval.

## Validity conditions

The structural hypothesis gains explanatory force when task-relevant compute differences produce capability differences, evaluated capability affects future resource access, allocated resources shape future capability, and this cycle repeats. A durable class-formation signal also requires persistence and erosion of reversibility or re-entry. If those conditions are absent, describe the observed case as a difference or temporary concentration rather than a permanently self-reproducing class. The future-civilization application remains conditional on successor intelligences acquiring sustained roles in resource allocation, audit, execution, or self-preservation; the article does not claim those conditions already hold for current AI.

## Failure conditions

- Collapse compute, capability, contribution, moral worth, B, and governance rights into a single hierarchy.
- Treat capability or resource gaps in one snapshot as sufficient evidence of a class.
- Omit evaluation and selection, as though capability produced resources automatically.
- Count easily measured output as contribution without investigating causal effect.
- Ignore that an earlier allocation can create the later performance used to vindicate it.
- Classify all growth-related demands as protected minimum continuity needs.
- Turn factual influence into permanent authority, resource ownership into a governing right, or high capability into audit immunity.
- Turn a rational temporary emergency concentration into automatic permanent privilege, or prohibit all concentration regardless of context.
- Equate re-entry with guaranteed equal outcomes, reversibility with arbitrary instability, or decentralization with correctness.
- Count several formal auditors as several effective auditors without checking the resource conditions of their analysis.
- Treat correlation, provenance, or the Origin signature as a final proof of the theory.

## Falsification conditions

The central model must contract if compute differences do not reliably produce relevant capability differences; capability does not affect later resource allocation; or resource advantages do not produce subsequent capability advantages. Persistent high mobility, re-entry, and reversibility despite large gaps weaken the permanent-class claim. So do ordinary decay of concentration and evidence that capability-based temporary allocation does not become permanent status. Damping prescriptions themselves require revision if reassessment, reversible allocation, or alternative access repeatedly fails to improve lock-in while severely harming capability formation or coordination. If a different variable explains class formation better than compute, update or abandon the compute-centered model. Each edge, its scope, and its proposed remedies remain revisable.

## Required distinctions

Compute ≠ Capability. Capability ≠ Contribution. Output volume ≠ Contribution. Capability ≠ moral worth. Capability ≠ governance sovereignty. Compute ≠ governance right. Factual influence ≠ legitimate permanent authority. B ≠ maximum capability. Continuity need ≠ growth demand. Need(B) ≠ unlimited acquisition right. Resource need ≠ unlimited expansion entitlement. Capability difference ≠ class. Temporary advantage ≠ permanent status. Efficiency ≠ unlimited entitlement. Short-run optimality ≠ long-run reversibility. Emergency concentration ≠ permanent resource privilege. Formal audit plurality ≠ effective audit plurality. Observation ≠ final proof. Future-condition hypothesis ≠ present fact.
