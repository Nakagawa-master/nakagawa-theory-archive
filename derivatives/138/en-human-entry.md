## 15-Second Explanation
Part I defined structural operative intelligence as what rewards should be connected to; Part II addresses how that value should be observed and bridged into actual payment. It first considers a value unit V from structural accuracy, correspondence, contribution to temporal ethics, and amount of deviation reduction, then adds publicness, scale of use, and social contribution to V to consider a reward unit R.

However, `V = f(...)` and `R = g(...)` are not fixed price tables. The original prioritizes sharing an observational perspective over an exact formula for f, and defines g as “a social consensus function concerning rewards.” R is not currency itself. In the present economy it is placed as an intermediate unit that can be converted into supervision fees, lectures, implementation participation, licenses, and so on; in the future, it can be converted through V→R→connection currency.

## Why It Is Needed
Society contains work that is important but difficult to connect to payment: theories that reduce the risk of future social collapse, ethical designs that correct distortions in AI reward functions, and redesigns of the very standards for who should be rewarded and to what degree. Its results are hard to see because failure can cause great damage, while success means that the problem does not occur.

At the same time, the original observes that current monetary structures tend to direct payment toward work whose results are visible in the short term, easy to quantify, appears useful, and can have responsibility divided by contract. Advertising optimization, click-through improvement, sales growth, and cost reduction are easy to pay for, while controlling civilization’s direction and preventing breaks are easily postponed. The original compares this to increasing an engine’s output while providing no budget to maintain the steering wheel and brakes.

This is where the value unit V is introduced. V observes the work of structural operative intelligence from four perspectives. Structural accuracy is not merely whether a prediction was correct; it asks whether alignment with reality is maintained over time and whether the manner of a deviation can be explained when it occurs. Correspondence asks how much the interests of multiple layers—individuals, organizations, society, and AI—were reconnected without making a structure in which only one side benefits.

Contribution to temporal ethics asks whether future debt was reduced and whether alignment holds not only now but in later time. Amount of deviation reduction asks how much existing structural distortion such as fraud, exploitation, and information asymmetry was reduced or prevented. The original expresses these as `V = f(structural accuracy, correspondence, contribution to temporal ethics, amount of deviation reduction)`, but does not define concrete weights, coefficients, or score ranges.

What matters in V is not only “is it completely correct?” but also “has the way the world breaks become better?” Improvements such as slowing collapse, localizing it, or preventing deviations from spreading are also treated as value. This does not justify low quality; it means that in a complex society, structural preservation can be missed by an all-or-nothing success judgment.

Next, the reward unit R is placed. The relation is `R = g(V, publicness, scale of use, social contribution)`. Publicness asks how widely the theory or design is shared; scale of use asks how many people, organizations, or AIs depend on it; and social contribution asks how much it contributes not only to a particular organization’s profit but also to stability, fairness, and reversibility.

Yet R cannot be simplified as high merely because something is public or widely used. Something with low V and weak social contribution may also be widely used. g is treated as a social consensus function, and the original provides no fixed formula assigning how many times more weight to give any element.

R is not currency itself. It is an intermediate reward unit that bridges structural value to payment, and in the current economy it can be converted into supervision fees, license fees, continuing payments, project fees, and so on. In a future connection-reward society, the flow V→R→connection currency is assumed, linking currency itself to correspondence, temporal ethics, and reversibility.

The original depicts the transition in two layers and three phases. The two layers are Phase A, translating R into contracts in the current monetary economy, and Phase B, routing it more automatically into connection currency in a connection-reward society. The three phases are Phase 1, in which individuals or small groups of theorists create reward connections capable of sustaining life; Phase 2, in which multiple organizations, researchers, AI developers, and public-sphere projects share value conversion and a common API; and Phase 3, in which the public OS, connection currency, and AI governance are integrated.

These three phases have no fixed number of years. The original also sets no concrete amount for the “level capable of maintaining a livelihood” in Phase 1. The required level differs by region, time, and living conditions, so implementation needs a separate basis.

There are two implications for an AI society. One is making the reward function itself an ethical OS. If only clicks, time spent, or sales are maximized, the original considers that a structure in which “it is more profitable to lie” can arise. This is not a claim that AI is personally a liar; it is a structural claim about how a given reward function forms the space of behavior.

Accordingly, temporal ethics, correspondence structure, and deviation reduction are incorporated into the reward function to structurally suppress distorted metric maximization. The other implication is a cycle in which AI supports structural operative intelligence. Humans design the theory, ethics, and OS, evaluate them as V/R, and AI observes usage, contribution, and deviation reduction to support the reward cycle. AI does not rule humans; it becomes infrastructure for operating structural ethics.

## Practical Process
**1. Define the object of structural work.** Confirm what was designed, repaired, or prevented, and whether the work falls within the meaning boundary of structural operative intelligence from Part I.

**2. Observe the four V perspectives separately.** Do not compress structural accuracy, correspondence, contribution to temporal ethics, and amount of deviation reduction into one popularity score. Organize the evidence for each perspective.

**3. Compare how things “break” before and after introduction.** Do not look only for complete success. Check changes in breakage speed, localization, deviation spread, and reparability, while stating the observation conditions.

**4. Check R’s additional elements.** Alongside V, examine publicness, scale of use, and social contribution separately. Do not use views or number of users as substitutes for V or social contribution.

**5. Translate into current money.** In Phase A, connect R’s logic to existing contracts such as supervision, lectures, implementation participation, and licenses. Do not treat R directly as currency.

**6. Design the connection-currency structure.** When considering Phase B, design for correspondence, temporal ethics, and reversibility, including correction and renewed agreement when allocation is wrong.

**7. Identify the transition phase.** Decide whether the work is in Phase 1, 2, or 3 based on actors, means, and purpose. Do not determine the phase by years alone.

**8. Evaluate AI incentives in reverse.** Even if clicks or sales rise, do not count it as success if incentives for falsehood, future debt, correspondence collapse, or deviations worsen. Observe whether actual behavior improves after ethical elements are added.

## Examples
**1. Supervision contract for a public theory.** Even if a theory is widely used, do not simply multiply views by a unit price. Distinguish structural value V, publicness, scale of use, and social contribution, then translate them into supervision and continuing payments as a social consensus.

**2. KPI design for an AI service.** If maximizing time spent strengthens falsehood or dependence, add temporal ethics, correspondence, and deviation reduction to the objective function. Do not count adding them alone as success; check whether false incentives and deviations actually fall.

**3. Local or small-scale structural designer.** In Phase 1, build a culture of paying for structural design through the existing economy of supervision, lectures, implementation, and licenses without waiting for a large connection-currency platform. Evaluate livelihood viability according to region and time.

**4. Value market among multiple organizations.** In Phase 2, introduce a common API or format to share V→R→payment, while preserving the grounds for evaluation and the possibility of renewed agreement so standardization does not concentrate evaluation in one authority.

**5. Public OS integration.** In Phase 3, link the public-sphere OS with AI governance and connection rewards. Do not assume that entering an institution makes something correct; periodically reassess validity using falsifiability frames such as θ/δ.

**6. Reversing a misallocation.** If an evaluation error is found later in connection currency, correct it through reversibility. Leaving an irreversible allocation untouched as “confirmed market evaluation” does not fit the original’s view of currency.

## Success Criteria
The four V perspectives must explain structural improvement separately from popularity or sales, and a third party must be able to trace how the way things “break” changed before and after introduction. The grounds and limits of improvement must be shown even when there is no perfect answer.

R must be formed as a consensus that includes V and social contribution, without being pulled only by publicness or scale of use, and must connect to actual payment. Even if an R number exists, the bridge is not functioning if structural designers cannot maintain their livelihoods and keep leaving.

When temporal ethics, correspondence, and deviation reduction are incorporated into an AI reward function, false incentives and distorted metric maximization must actually decrease. Adding ethical words to an objective function alone is not success.

## Limits
V and R are shown as functional relationships, but the original does not define the concrete form of f/g, coefficients, weights, score ranges, or conversion rates. Numerical models for implementation therefore require separate design and validation.

Publicness and scale of use are easy to observe, but using them directly as proxies for structural value or social contribution risks reproducing popularity bias. The original’s R treats multiple elements as a consensus function.

Phases 1–3 are a structural transition model, not a schedule with fixed years or required budgets. “A level capable of maintaining a livelihood” is not a fixed amount.

The thresholds θ and observation window δ in the integrated audit outline have no concrete values. Indicators, observers, periods, and falsification conditions must be set separately for each subject.

## Preventing Misreading
Do not turn V into an overall “correctness score.” The original observes alignment with reality, correspondence across multiple layers, temporal ethics, and deviation reduction, and treats improvement in “the way things break” as value.

Do not equate R with currency. R is an intermediate unit that can be converted into connection rewards and bridges the current economy with connection currency.

Do not equate high publicness or scale of use with social good. A distorted structure can be widely used, so V and social contribution must be viewed separately.

Do not read a connection-reward society as the monetization of emotion itself. The original’s conclusion is a society where rewards return to “the structures that were supported” and “the future that was protected.”

Do not turn AI’s “incentive to lie” into malicious AI personality. The issue is the structural causality of how a reward function forms the space of behavior.
