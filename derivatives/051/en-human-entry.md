## 15-Second Explanation
The parent source presents a vision for evolving sales from an individual skill that only a few highly capable people can reproduce into an institution that accumulates trust and circulates it through organizations and society. It observes resonance, continuity, and referrals—not only revenue and contract counts—as traces of trust capital, and connects the individual's “form,” organizational evaluation and incentives, and social transparency. However, the source does not aim to reduce trust completely to numbers or turn sales conversations into a fixed script.

## Why It Becomes Necessary
When sales depends heavily on individual experience and intuition, it can produce outstanding results, while making it difficult for an organization to inherit what that person saw, in what order they made judgments, and why trust arose with the other party. The source regards this personalization as “both a strength and a vulnerability,” and externalizes the structure of judgment as a reproducible “form.”

It also argues that when conventional sales evaluation is biased toward sales amount and contract count, the relational resources behind the results disappear from view. Resonance, in which the other party feels able to entrust the matter; continuity, which does not end after a single occasion; and referrals, which make someone want to tell a third party, accumulate as trust capital distinct from sales. To handle these institutionally, they must be translated into observable traces such as re-contract and repeat rates, referral counts, and emotional feedback after meetings.

However, the source does not say that “making it numerical solves the problem.” Some parts of trust cannot be quantified, institutions can become rigid, and incentives can produce the opposite effect. Therefore, the goal of institutionalization is not complete control, but transparency that makes the grounds for trust formation visible and allows correction when short-term results and long-term trust diverge.

## Practical Process
**1. Externalize the order of judgment.** Share not the top performer’s phrasing, but what they saw and how they judged it, including the order of presenting the product, benefits, and price, and the flow of questions, silence, and resonance.

**2. Observe trust capital separately from sales.** Place multiple measures side by side, including re-contract and repeat rates, referral counts, and records of emotion, understanding, and reassurance after meetings.

**3. Review the evaluation system.** Add long-term relationships, resonance, referrals, and continuing trust as evaluation axes instead of evaluating only new contracts and short-term sales.

**4. Check for incentive reversal.** See whether strong rewards for short-term results have damaged trust or dialogue with customers.

**5. Prevent institutional rigidity.** Periodically review whether forms and indicators have become ends in themselves and whether the context of the field and living dialogue remain.

**6. Extend it to the social level.** Through trust indicators with public relevance and public, verifiable dialogue systems, treat sales as an activity that forms social capital.

## Examples of Application
**1. Sales education.** Teach not “say this phrase and it will sell,” but where the other party’s state was observed, and when the salesperson asked, explained, or remained silent.

**2. Sales evaluation.** Even if sales increase, if re-contracts and referrals fall and understanding or reassurance after meetings worsens, read this as a mismatch between short-term results and long-term trust.

**3. Organizational incentives.** If a system that strongly rewards only new contracts induces unreasonable proposals, redesign evaluation to include long-term relationships.

**4. Social trust indicators.** When sharing trust across an industry, make the structure public and verifiable—what is measured and in what context it has meaning—rather than using a single credit score.

## How to Read the Numbers
The parent source gives examples of measuring re-contract and repeat rates as a “continuing trust index” and referral counts as an “externally transmitted trust index,” and gives an example of qualitatively recording emotional feedback after meetings through a simple survey or AI analysis. However, it does not define a fixed threshold such as a passing score of a certain percentage or higher.

The integrated audit summary at the end of the source also contains a system of symbols such as trust accumulation rate `g`, cancellation rate `C`, SLA compliance rate `A`, RB success rate `R`, MTTR, phase stability `S`, threshold `θ`, and observation window `δ`. No concrete values are assigned to these; they should be read as the source’s falsification and revision structure, not as general sales KPIs in the main text.

The key point is reversal evaluation. Even if re-contract rates or referral counts increase, if the other party’s freedom, dialogue transparency, or relationship quality worsens, that cannot be equated with an improvement in trust capital.

## Success Criteria
The reasons for individual success are shared as a structure of judgment, and another representative can reproduce the same way of thinking.

Sales and contract counts are observed separately from traces of trust capital: resonance, continuity, and referrals.

Evaluation and incentives align with long-term trust as well as short-term sales, and achieving the indicators does not conceal damage to trust.

Even when expanded to the social level, trust is not reduced to a single score; transparency is increased as a public, verifiable dialogue system.

## Limitations
Trust cannot be fully quantified. No matter how many indicators are established, they cannot completely replace relational context, the other party’s judgment, or the quality of dialogue.

Institutions can become rigid. If a form becomes a script and indicators become an achievement game, they can erode the flexibility and creativity in sales that the source seeks to protect.

Incentives can produce the opposite effect. Strongly rewarding short-term results may improve the apparent numbers while damaging long-term trust.

## Misreading Prevention
“Institutionalizing sales” does not mean fixing conversations into a manual. What is shared is the order of judgment and the points of observation.

“Making trust capital visible” does not mean reducing trust to one score. Re-contracts, referrals, emotional feedback, and so on are separate proxy observations.

“Prioritizing long-term relationships” does not mean maintaining every relationship unconditionally. The source centers on the sustainability of trust, not continuity of the numbers by itself.

“Making sales an institution of civilization” is not a proposal for a centralized credit-rating system. It means extending sales toward indicators with public relevance, measures of social value, and public, verifiable dialogue systems.
