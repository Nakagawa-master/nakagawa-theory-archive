## 15-Second Explanation
When moving to a new institution, stopping the old institution first can cut off the functions of daily life and rights as well. Therefore, operate old and new in parallel for a certain period, expand only the range the new system can genuinely replace, and scale down the old system while retaining a way back in case of failure. This is “orderly replacement.”

## Why It Is Needed
Social institutions, corporate systems, value evaluation, and agreement-forming mechanisms do not operate on ideals alone. Current systems have real functions supporting daily life, including settlement, employment, guarantees, relief, contracts, and division of responsibility.

Even if a new mechanism appears more desirable, immediately stopping the old system may also eliminate those real functions. Conversely, keeping the old mechanism forever means the transition will not finish. What is needed is not “what to preserve,” but **changing the replacement ratio while observing what the new system has actually replaced**.

## Central Structure
```text
The current system performs indispensable real functions
↓
Immediate switching carries a risk of rupture
↓
Operate old and new in parallel
↓
Separate responsibility, scope of application, and exceptions
↓
Observe the new system’s actual replacement capacity
↓
Use defects, disadvantage, and opposition as corrective input
↓
Retain rollback and relief
↓
Increase the new-system ratio only for the range already replaced
↓
Scale down dependence on the old system in the range where dependence has fallen
```

## Practical Process
1. **Inventory current functions.** Confirm what the old institution actually supports.
2. **Specify what the new system replaces.** Do not leave the replacement target ambiguous.
3. **Define the boundary of dual operation.** Decide the responsibility, target, and exceptions for each.
4. **Run it in practice.** Confirm through actual operation whether the new system functions.
5. **Collect failure and opposition.** Do not discard defects or disadvantage as “resistance”; use them as design information.
6. **Keep a way back.** Prepare relief, rollback, and alternative routes.
7. **Advance only what can be replaced.** Raise the replacement rate by function, not through blanket transition.
8. **Gradually scale down the old system.** Do not stop the old system before confirming the new system’s capacity.

## Examples of Application
### Corporate systems
Do not stop the old system on the same day; compare for a certain period whether the new system can reproduce important operations. Keep an escape route for failures and scale down the old system after core functions stabilize.

### Institutional reform
Separate the legal fact that a new institution has begun from the fact that people can actually reach rights, benefits, and relief. If users suffer disadvantage, revise the transition design.

### New value evaluation
When introducing new value axes such as connection or contribution, do not ignore the settlement and life-maintenance functions carried by money. Move while confirming the range in which the new evaluation can withstand real exchange and guarantees.

## Success Test
- The functions replaced by the new system can be explained in substance.
- The responsibility boundary between old and new is clear.
- Relief during defects is actually usable.
- Opposition and anxiety connect to design improvement.
- Dependence on the old system has substantively decreased.
- Dual operation has end conditions.
- Stopping the old system has not preceded confirmation of the new system’s capacity.

## Limitations
Dual operation is not a panacea. It may increase duplicate costs, responsibility conflicts, user confusion, and data consistency problems. Therefore, do not regard “leaving both” itself as a safety measure; keep boundaries, observation, relief, and end conditions together.

This derivative also does not fix a specific transition period or replacement rate. The loss in failure, rights protection, and technical reversibility differ by target.

## Misreading Prevention
- “Orderly replacement” is not a way to postpone reform.
- “Dual operation” does not mean making a dual system permanent.
- “Rollback possible” does not mean a lack of confidence in the new institution.
- Opposition is not always correct, but it can become input for finding design defects.
- An increase in new-system users alone does not mean replacement is complete.
