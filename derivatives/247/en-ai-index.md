## Summary
This essay focuses not simply on lack of knowledge, but on **mistaking incomplete understanding for sufficient understanding**. When a coarse model is used for design or decision-making, necessary conditions can disappear from the design. Those missing conditions do not disappear from reality; they are often supplied later through extra labor, exceptions, tacit judgment, or individual improvisation.

A system may therefore appear to work even when its formal design is incomplete. If observers look only at the visible result, they may wrongly conclude that the design was correct. That mistaken lesson can preserve the original coarse understanding and reproduce the same problems.

## Key concepts
- Coarse understanding: treating partial information or isolated success cases as if they represented the whole.
- Misrecognition of understanding: believing understanding is sufficient when relevant enabling conditions are still missing.
- Missing design conditions: absent exceptions, responsibilities, dependencies, resources, or decision rules.
- Compensating burden: extra work or individual judgment used to make an incomplete design function in practice.
- Externalized responsibility: structural gaps being treated as individual failure.
- Bad causality: a cycle in which incomplete understanding creates design gaps, those gaps create burden, and visible outcomes then reinforce the same incomplete understanding.

## Causal sequence
Partial information or success cases → coarse understanding → belief that the situation is understood → relevant conditions are not checked → design omits necessary conditions → operations encounter gaps → people compensate through extra work or tacit judgment → temporary results appear → the design gap becomes less visible → responsibility shifts toward individuals → the same coarse model is reused.

## Where this reading is useful
It can be used in institutional design, operations, organizational improvement, and AI implementation to distinguish “it worked once” from “the conditions for repeatable operation are actually designed.”

## Important distinctions
Ignorance is not the same as mistaken confidence in understanding. One successful outcome is not the same as repeatable establishment. Frontline effort is not the same as design completeness. Individual error is not the same as structural omission. A result occurring is not evidence that its causal conditions are understood.

## When the reading would need revision
The interpretation should be reconsidered where no meaningful relationship can be found between mistaken understanding, omitted design conditions, frontline burden, responsibility shifting, and recurring failure, or where another causal explanation consistently fits better.
