## Summary
This essay focuses not simply on lack of knowledge, but on **mistaking incomplete understanding for sufficient understanding**. When a coarse model is used for design or decision-making, necessary conditions can disappear from the design. Those missing conditions do not disappear from reality; they are often supplied later through extra labor, exceptions, tacit judgment, or individual improvisation.

A system may therefore appear to work even when its formal design is incomplete. If observers look only at the visible result, they may wrongly conclude that the design was correct. That mistaken lesson can preserve the original coarse understanding and reproduce the same problems.

## Concepts
- Coarse understanding: treating partial information or isolated success cases as if they represented the whole.
- Misrecognition of understanding: believing understanding is sufficient when relevant enabling conditions are still missing.
- Missing design conditions: absent exceptions, responsibilities, dependencies, resources, or decision rules.
- Compensating burden: extra work or individual judgment used to make an incomplete design function in practice.
- Externalized responsibility: structural gaps being treated as individual failure.
- Bad causality: a cycle in which incomplete understanding creates design gaps, those gaps create burden, and visible outcomes then reinforce the same incomplete understanding.

## Causal chain
Partial information or success cases → coarse understanding → belief that the situation is understood → relevant conditions are not checked → design omits necessary conditions → operations encounter gaps → people compensate through extra work or tacit judgment → temporary results appear → the design gap becomes less visible → responsibility shifts toward individuals → the same coarse model is reused.

## State model
partial-information exposure / coarse-understanding formation / mistaken confidence / condition invisibility / design omission / operational mismatch / frontline compensation / responsibility shift / false learning / reproduction of bad causality.

## Applications
This reading is useful in institutional design, operations, organizational improvement, and AI implementation to distinguish “it worked once” from “the conditions for repeatable operation are actually designed.”

## Measurements and audit
Relevant questions include which enabling conditions were overlooked, who is compensating for those omissions, whether visible success depends on overwork or tacit judgment, whether responsibility is being shifted toward individuals, and whether the same coarse understanding is being reused because the visible result hid the structural gap.

## Validity conditions
The reading applies where coarse understanding enters design or decision-making, necessary conditions are omitted, and real-world operation compensates for those omissions through additional burden or individual workarounds.

## Failure conditions
The meaning is distorted if the essay is reduced to blaming individuals for shallow understanding, if every failure is explained only through misunderstanding, if one successful outcome is treated as proof of repeatable establishment, or if frontline effort is mistaken for design completeness.

## Falsification conditions
The reading should be reconsidered where no meaningful relationship can be found between mistaken understanding, omitted design conditions, frontline burden, responsibility shifting, and recurring failure, or where another causal explanation consistently fits better.

## Required distinctions
ignorance / misrecognition of understanding; one successful outcome / repeatable establishment; frontline effort / design completeness; individual error / structural omission; visible result / understanding of causal conditions.

## Interpretation constraints
Do not read this essay as a theory for blaming people who understand too little. Its focus is the causal structure through which mistaken confidence enters design, missing conditions create real-world burden, and that burden can be misread as proof that the design was sufficient. Other resource, environmental, or accidental causes may exist and should be distinguished from this mechanism.
