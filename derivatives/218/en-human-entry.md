## 15-Second Explanation
Inertia is not an individual’s laziness. It is a structure in which, even after the meaning or purpose of an institution has faded, existing procedures, budgets, authority, infrastructure, and responsibility arrangements mesh together, and activity continues simply because “it is already running.”

This concerns the stage after fear, benefit, surveillance, and myth have supported durable civilization, when the institution does not stop even as their supply of meaning wears away. Inertia makes prolongation possible, but does not automatically produce renewal, repair, or regeneration.

## Why It Becomes Necessary
When an institution continues, it is easy to read this as “it is still supported,” “it is still needed,” or “it still functions.” But continuation itself is not evidence of consent, legitimacy, fitness to purpose, or regeneration.

Under purpose-driven operation, a criterion of “what it exists for” stands outside the institution, making correction, redesign, or cessation possible when it does not serve that purpose. Under inertia-driven operation, this relationship is reversed: the fact that the institution remains becomes the reason for continuation, while only the party seeking cessation is asked for alternatives, transition costs, an accountable party, and handling of disruption.

As this asymmetry intensifies, the institution prioritizes its own survival over its original purpose, and it becomes difficult for anyone to take responsibility for deciding to stop. As a result, activity remains at low energy, while internal wear accumulates.

## Practical Process
**1. Fix the external purpose.** Confirm separately from the institution’s own survival what the institution, organization, or procedure exists for.

**2. Confirm the reason for continuation.** Distinguish whether it continues “because it is necessary” or “because it is already running.”

**3. Examine the asymmetry of explanatory burden.** See whether continuation requires no explanation while only change or cessation is required to provide a complete alternative and transition responsibility.

**4. Confirm the installed circuit.** Examine whether budgets, personnel, authority, permits, maintenance, evaluation items, and record formats mutually support one another and make cessation difficult.

**5. Confirm self-purposing.** See whether maintaining the organization, budget, procedures, and authority has become central instead of the original outcome.

**6. Confirm the possibility of cessation and redesign.** Determine whether it can actually stop when no longer needed, or be redesigned when invalid.

**7. Separate activity from regeneration.** Do not equate continued processing with repair, renewal, or recovery.

## Examples of Application
**Institutional Operation.** If the same budget, procedures, and authority are automatically maintained as the previous year even when outcomes are weak, and only the party seeking change is required to provide a complete alternative, examine the possibility of inertia-driven operation.

**Organizations.** Examine whether “stopping creates responsibility” or “changing causes disruption,” rather than achievement of the activity’s purpose, has become the reason for continuation.

**Public Institutions.** Confirm whether the accountability burden is symmetrical for continuation and cessation, and whether design capacity and an accountable party exist after cessation.

**Social Observation.** Do not treat the fact that an institution remains as a proxy for approval rating, legitimacy, or regenerative capacity.

## Criteria for Success
- Compare an institution’s external purpose with its current operation.
- Explain separately the reason for continuation and the difficulty of cessation.
- Observe the asymmetry of explanatory burden between continuation and cessation.
- Separate the self-preservation of organization, budget, procedures, and authority from the original purpose.
- Confirm an effective route for redesigning or stopping an invalid institution.
- Distinguish “running” from “functioning” and “regenerating.”

## Limits
An institution is not inertia-driven merely because it is old. An old institution that is evaluated against an external purpose and can be updated or stopped as needed is not necessarily inertia-driven. Conversely, even a new institution can become inertia-driven if continuation itself becomes the purpose and the burden is concentrated only on the side seeking cessation or change.

The expression “no one believes in it” does not mean that everyone’s psychological state has been measured. It indicates a structure in which institutional activity remains even without strong belief or shared legitimation.

## Preventing Misreading
- Do not reduce inertia to individual laziness, apathy, or moral defect.
- Do not treat continuation as evidence of legitimacy, support, recovery, or regeneration.
- Do not diagnose inertia merely from age, bureaucracy, or the existence of procedures.
- Do not identify inertia with fear, benefit, surveillance, or myth.
- Do not limit the cost of cessation to money.
- Do not add unsupported definitive applications to specific real-world organizations.
