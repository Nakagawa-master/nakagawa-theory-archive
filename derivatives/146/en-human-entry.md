## 15-Second Explanation

This structural theory reads “removing from insurance” not as a mere change in medicine prices, but as a question of whether the public access that the medical system originally sought to protect has separated from the burdens and inequalities that actually result. Even if a reform produces fiscal effects, a fissure forms between institutional origin and operating result if necessary care becomes harder to reach and burden differences or distrust expand.

The original understands this fissure through “contradiction-consumption structural theory” and “structural origin defense.” An institution can continue while carrying contradictions, but when they begin to damage its capacity for self-repair, what supported stability can reverse into a catalyst for breakdown.

## Why It Is a Structural Fissure

Medical insurance must both remain financially sustainable and let people who need care reach it. The two do not always coincide perfectly; institutions have continued by adjusting that mismatch.

But if changing coverage moves costs to users, and income or usage conditions divide the ability to receive care or buy medicines, while the institution's stated rationality moves far from people's experience, the problem exceeds a simple cost transfer. The correspondence between the origin purpose—public medical access—and actual effects breaks.

The source's expressions “a hole made by an ant” and “a hole in immunity” do not mean one change guarantees institutional collapse. They warn against missing the possibility that small boundary changes weaken self-repair when they spread in the same direction and continuously change burden, access, and trust.

## Practical Process

**1. Fix the change.** Confirm what moves from covered to uncovered, the target, timing, and alternatives.

**2. Track burden movement.** Do not look only at whether public spending falls; track where costs and benefits move among users, medical practice, and the market.

**3. Observe substantive access.** Separate formal ability to purchase from real access when price or conditions of continued use prevent people who need care from using it.

**4. Compare origin and result.** Check whether the institutional origin of public medical access corresponds to burden, inequality, trust, and sustainability after the change.

**5. Check one event or a chain.** Do not declare total collapse from one change; observe whether similar removals spread during the observation period.

**6. Judge reversal.** Even if fiscal indicators improve, do not call the whole institution successful if access gaps or long-term debt worsen.

**7. Revise the hypothesis when needed.** Narrow the fissure hypothesis if access, fairness, and trust remain stable; reassess design if deterioration continues.

## Examples of Application

**Changes to insurance coverage for OTC-like medicines.** Compare not only medicine prices but continuing out-of-pocket costs, income differences, movement to alternatives, and care-seeking behavior.

**Fiscal consolidation.** Separate short-term savings in benefits from changes in other medical costs, living burdens, and institutional trust caused by reduced access.

**Gradual removal from coverage.** Even if each change is limited, observe how the institutional boundary changes over time when similar changes spread across areas.

**Comparing explanations with practice.** Check whether the institution's stated purpose corresponds to results observed by patients and medical workers.

## Success Criteria

Success cannot be judged only by fiscal burden. Observe substantive access to necessary care, distribution of user burden, trust in the institution, long-term sustainability, and correspondence with institutional origin as a bundle.

The original uses θ (threshold), δ (observation window), and M (phenomenon requiring reassessment), but fixes no numerical values or periods. In practice, state who measures what and for how long, and what the values do not guarantee.

## Limits

The original does not decide whether an individual should take, stop, or change a particular medicine. Items removed from coverage and current institutional conditions change over time, so concrete decisions require primary-source confirmation.

It does not probabilistically guarantee that removing coverage always expands access inequality. A structural fissure is an observational hypothesis that must be narrowed or revised using post-change data.

## Misreading Prevention

Removal from insurance ≠ immediate institutional collapse.

Fiscal improvement ≠ improvement of the whole institution.

Higher out-of-pocket cost ≠ the same impact on every user.

Formal ability to purchase ≠ substantive medical access.

Contradiction consumption ≠ recommendation to leave contradictions unattended.

Structural origin defense ≠ permanent freezing of the current system.

θ / δ / M ≠ fixed universal numbers.

Evaluative language in a cited article ≠ a factual finding about medical providers generally.
