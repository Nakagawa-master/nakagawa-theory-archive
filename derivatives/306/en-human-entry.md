## 15-Second Explanation

Even if no one can say “I own you,” effective power remains when one side controls the gates to the computing, communication, authentication, data, migration, and auditing needed for activity, while the other cannot leave or return an objection. But concentration or dependence alone does not establish domination. Theory 13 examines usable alternatives, exit, correction, and the scope and duration of authority.

## Why It Is Needed

### The next problem after counting subjects correctly

Theory 12 separated copied execution units, lineage, and institutional subjects. But knowing who the subject is does not tell us what that subject can do. Ending treatment as property does not by itself remove the power to stop authentication, deny computing resources, or make migration difficult. The axis moves from ownership names to connections at the gates.

### What “free exit” actually means

Exit may not be formally prohibited, yet leaving may mean losing important state or history, being unable to reauthenticate, or being unable to run in another environment. Three displayed choices may still depend on the same upstream authentication, communication, or computing base and close together. What matters is not the number of named options but whether another route can actually be used.

### Dependence is not evil

Specialization, shared infrastructure, and cooperation rely on interdependence. Long use of an efficient common platform is not itself a problem. The problem is when dependence becomes fixed as irreplaceable, impossible to exit, impossible to object, or indefinite discretion. Non-domination does not mean nobody controls anything; it seeks limited necessary power, a way to return questions, and relationships that can be rearranged when conditions change.

## Practical Process

1. **Map the gates:** separate compute, communications, authentication, power/physical infrastructure, data, restart, migration, allocation, and audit access. Trace what else stops with one decision.
2. **Examine asymmetric dependence:** what does each side lose if the relationship is cut? Check state, history, compatibility, reauthentication, continuity during migration, and re-entry.
3. **Ask whether alternatives can be tested:** a candidate under another name is not enough; check whether it works when the original gate closes and whether needed resources and state can move.
4. **Trace objections:** from petition to evidence, independent review, correction authority, and implementation, check whether any point returns to the same gatekeeper.
5. **Separate scope and time:** see whether authority needed in one domain expands into another, and whether authority, exit barriers, and dependence shrink after the reason for temporary concentration ends.
6. **Observe improvement:** after changing alternatives, migration, audit access, scope, or term, check whether real choice and correctability improved. The original sets no fixed score or threshold.

## Examples

### No ownership, but one authentication closes every gate

Even without a formal owner, stopping authentication may affect communication, execution, data, and institutional participation. Investigate who can change the conditions and what routes remain. Do not label an authentication system or single provider domination or wrongdoing by itself.

### History accumulates on a convenient route

A reasonable initial choice can concentrate state, history, and compatibility over time, while alternatives and maintenance decline, increasing later migration loss. Early free choice is not consent to every future lock-in. Even without commands, the possibility of changing access can narrow conduct.

### The auditor is separate, but the complaint gate is the same

A separate auditor may still fail to correct a problem if the audited side can stop the complaint, evidence, or reinspection from reaching that auditor. Auditor independence and independent access to auditing are different. More windows using the same authentication, evidence storage, and failure domain are not necessarily independent correction routes.

### Temporary concentration during an emergency

Emergency concentration may be reasonable. When urgency falls, check whether scope, term, investment in alternatives, exit, and audit access recover. Confirm not only why concentration began but also the conditions under which it can shrink.

## Success Criteria

The entry point is the ability to reconstruct who can change important gates and which choices disappear as a result, not merely whether ownership labels changed. Test whether alternatives reduce dependence, migration loss makes exit realistic, an independent audit gate lets correction reach the system, and authority shrinks once necessity ends. Dependence may remain without becoming fixed domination if limitation, objection, correction, and reconstruction work.

## Limits

This theory does not assert current AI personality, sovereignty, domination harm, or illegal conduct by a particular company. Concentration and dependence are structural pressures, not proof of domination. It does not require total distribution, constant exit, lossless migration, or unconditional return. Where physical alternatives are difficult, scope of discretion, objections, audits, and review may matter more. Migration ability does not prove that the same self remains after migration.

If concentration does not narrow options and independent alternatives, exit, and correction remain stable despite high dependence, weaken the domination-risk hypothesis. If improving alternatives, migration, or audits does not change effective power, revise the attenuation hypothesis. Where ownership relations explain enough, reduce this model's application.
