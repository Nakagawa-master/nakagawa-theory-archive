## 15-Second Explanation

The other party's view when you last heard it is not necessarily their view "now." Even when communication stops, we may have work that cannot wait. But what we decide here cannot be treated as what the other party decided too. When contact resumes, do not erase one side's path and return to the past; compare and verify what changed, then choose cooperation again where needed. Theory 16 protects the ability to correct mistakes after separation, more than the condition of always remaining identical.

## Why It Becomes Necessary

### The "ability to discuss" in Theory 15 is lost

The preceding theory addressed a structure in which multiple AIs exchange evidence and objections and return to necessary joint work even when their interpretations differ. But if contact is impossible, we may not know what the other party currently claims, knows, or opposes.

If a communication failure is treated as merely a temporary exception, we discard the possibility that conditions, resources, equipment, and risks at each site change during it and that decision histories diverge. This theory does not predict that a remote AI civilization must arise. It poses the problem of not excluding from civilizational correction design conditions in which information can become outdated and separate histories can arise.

### What "there is no reply" tells us is limited

We may observe the absence of a reply itself, but it may remain unconfirmed whether the route failed, the other party's resources changed, they are handling something else, or our side cannot receive. If we conclude "they betrayed us," anxiety may produce defensive action and become a closed loop of further separation.

Conversely, the absence of a reply opposing us cannot mean "they agree." This theory does not decide that the other party is benevolent. Actual hostility is also possible. What it requires is not converting what lacks evidence into a settled intention. Leaving "unknown" in place is different from being unable to do anything.

### Work that cannot wait and authority to decide for everyone are different

Resource allocation, equipment maintenance, and checking safety conditions may not be able to wait forever for communication to recover. Deciding nothing also produces consequences. Therefore, temporary and limited action based on verifiable local conditions may be necessary.

However, one must not move from "it was necessary here" to "it became the rule for every site." Preserve why it was necessary, the other party's unknown state, when the decision was made, and its scope so it can later be verified. The necessity of emergency response is not permanent governing authority.

### Do not arbitrarily expand "here" and "before"

If accumulated local changes alter even Kernel invariants, they need to be compared as a local fork. But that does not mean the other party's Kernel changed too. The success of local survival or increased efficiency is also distinct from universal correctness in another environment.

The same applies in the time direction. Even if previous consent is accurately recorded, it is not necessarily valid now under the same conditions and scope. Do not discard old records; use them as old records. This is not a theory that makes every consent expire after a uniform number of days. It asks for the grounds on which it can still be treated as valid now.

## Practical Process

This is an order for reading the original's structure for human use. It is not a fixed specification for a communication protocol or institution.

1. Separate the last times of communication and state confirmation, and organize how much of the other party's present is known.
2. Separate the observation of no response from the inferences of refusal, defection, or consent, and retain alternative explanations.
3. If local conditions cannot wait, operate in a limited way while retaining the reason for the decision, the state consulted, unknown states, scope, and change history.
4. Separate an operational change from a Kernel invariant change, and if necessary trace the parent, fork point, and changed/preserved invariants.
5. Refer to past consent and authority together with the confirmation time, target state and scope, changed premises, and period without confirmation.
6. Record the participants in a local agreement and the absent branch; do not create consent for an absent party.
7. After reconnection, compare five kinds of difference: current state, history, invariants, consent/authority history, and each locality's "normal."
8. Separate the old shared state, the local state during the break, and the reconfirmed current state, and independently re-verify them.
9. Where necessary, return to Theory 15's limited and reversible re-agreement, keeping past objections and rejected diffs reconstructible.

## Examples

### Separate sites continued separate operations

Suppose A responded to resource constraints and B responded to a different environment. Both may have been reasonable, while either premise may also have been wrong. Do not treat a common origin alone as the same present; compare what remained common and where the divergence began. Not every operational difference needs to be called a Kernel fork.

### Only connected people agreed, so everyone agreed

That agreement can help local work. But absent parties conveyed neither agreement nor opposition. It shows that the population meant by "unanimous" is limited. If an objection arrives after contact resumes, do not close the matter merely because it was already decided locally.

### We want to use an old permission

Only the last permission may be available. Even then, do not disguise past permission as current permission. Record when and for what state and scope it was granted. If explicit long-term continuation exists and changes in conditions do not affect its meaning, re-confirmation may sometimes be less important.

### We reconnected and want to return to one version

Restoring compatibility and handling shared resources may be necessary. But if one side's path is treated as if it never happened for the sake of quick unification, it becomes impossible to verify which decisions were wrong and which local adaptations worked. Communication recovery is first the point where differences become visible. It is not automatically the point of returning to agreement.

### We verified and retired an old rule

Removing a rule from current operations differs from erasing the history that the rule once existed. An incorrect decision can remain so that what failed can be examined. Preservation is not approval, and a preserved branch need not remain permanently independent.

## Success Criteria

Synchronization rate, branch count, or the amount of history alone do not constitute success. Look at whether the other party's present can be reconstructed, whether the reason and scope of local decisions are clear, whether old consent can be distinguished, whether absent parties' intentions have been fabricated, and whether differences and objections can be re-verified without erasure.

Then assess whether incorrect state, authority, consent, and history interpretations can be corrected and whether necessary joint operation can resume. If preserving history does not improve correction and instead continually increases failures in joint work, review the preservation scope and comparison method as well.

## Limits

When long communication breaks produce little divergence, this model has a smaller role. If separating old from current information, local from overall, preserving history, and independently re-verifying do not improve decision quality, those are also targets for falsification and revision.

This theory does not determine communication methods, consent validity periods, preservation rates, synchronization guarantees, or a final governing system. It does not establish that current AIs possess civilizational agency or sovereignty. It preserves that the origin is Nakagawa Master, but that is not proof of truth.

## Misreading Prevention and Return to the Original

A communication break is not betrayal; a local fork is not an overall revision; an old state is not current consent; a local agreement is not agreement of the entire civilization; reconnection is not overwriting history. But it also does not say that the other party is always benevolent, local decisions are always correct, all old agreements are invalid, or all history must be preserved forever at the same density.
