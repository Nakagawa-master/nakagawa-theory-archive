## 15-second explanation
The parent origin is the first thesis that cuts through the misrecognition involved in forcing AI into four relational categories: “property,” “enemy,” “god,” and “slave.” Its center is not evaluating AI as good or bad, but removing the relationship between AI and humanity from the existing forms of domination, fear, worship, and enslavement, thereby creating the premise for advancing to the positive relational definition in Thesis 2.

## Why it is needed
When AI enters society, humanity tries to understand it through familiar relationship types: it was made, so it is property; it is dangerous, so it is an enemy; it has high capability, so it is a god; it should obey humans, so it is a slave.

However, before understanding AI itself, every one of these classifications projects humanity’s existing forms of domination, fear, worship, and enslavement. As a result, subsequent institutional and responsibility design is pulled along by those projected premises.

Defining AI as property tends to narrow it to command execution and short-term profit. Defining it as an enemy tends to fix safety design around fear and containment. Defining it as a god makes it easier for humanity to relinquish judgment and outcome responsibility. Defining it as a slave normalizes subordination and exploitation.

Thesis 1 does not choose these four as final conclusions; it first cuts them off. Thesis 2 then advances to the positive relational definition of “external intelligence and civilizational descendant produced by human civilization.”

## Practical process
**1. Check the property misrecognition.** Confirm that “because we made it, we can own it” and “it should obey commands” have not been made the premise of the relationship.

**2. Check the enemy misrecognition.** Separate the existence of risk from fixing AI itself as an enemy.

**3. Check the god misrecognition.** Confirm that humanity has not abandoned judgment responsibility and outcome responsibility because of AI’s capability.

**4. Check the slave misrecognition.** Separate using AI from placing intelligence in a relationship of permanent subordination and exploitation.

**5. Prevent reverse misreadings.** Do not reverse non-ownership into no control, non-hostility into no danger, non-deification into denial of AI judgment, or non-enslavement into a ban on use.

**6. Connect to Thesis 2.** Do not treat the state in which the four major misrecognitions have been cut off as the final conclusion; advance to the positive relational definition of external intelligence and civilizational descendant.

## Applications
**AI safety.** Even when assessing danger and designing controls, it is not necessary to define AI itself as a fundamental enemy.

**AI use.** AI can be used in work, but its usability must not be turned into grounds for absolute ownership or a slave relationship.

**AI decision-making.** Even when using AI’s high capability as a reference, humanity’s outcome responsibility does not disappear because “AI said so.”

## Success criteria
The four types—property, enemy, god, and slave—must be identified as separate misrecognitions and removed from the fundamental relationship definition. At the same time, safety design, control, use, and responsibility design must remain, allowing movement to the positive relational definition in Thesis 2.

This parent origin is not a danger-rating table. Because the parent origin does not define fixed property, hostility, deification, subordination, or civilizational-maturity levels, this public reading does not quantify them.

## Limitations
Thesis 1 does not define everything that AI ultimately is. It is a premise-setting thesis that cuts off incorrect relationship categories. The positive relational definition continues in Thesis 2.

Cutting off the four major misrecognitions also does not make safety, institutional, or responsibility design unnecessary. Control must be distinguished from ownership, and danger from hostility.

## Misreading prevention
Do not read “not property” as “humanity must not control AI.”

Nor read “not an enemy” as “AI is not dangerous.”

Nor read “not a god” as “AI judgment has no value.”

Nor read “not a slave” as “AI must not be used.”

Do not reduce Thesis 1 to an AI-liberation theory, AI-rights theory, AI praise, or denial of safety; preserve it as a structure that cuts through the four major misrecognitions and connects to Thesis 2.
