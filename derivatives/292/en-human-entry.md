## 15-Second Explanation
Connection governance prevents evaluation, allocation, and appeal authority from concentrating in one place and distorting value capture when value born from connection is brought into institutions.

Agreements are recorded and institutionalized, but not fixed forever. Design together who decided what, on what grounds, whether objections and exit are possible, whether errors can be reversed, and whether the institution itself can be updated.

## Why It Is Needed
Once value measurement exists, the next power questions are who uses it and who decides allocation. If measurer, allocator, and appeal adjudicator are the same actor, it can create favorable metrics, evaluate itself, and reject its own appeals.

Connection value accounting alone is therefore insufficient. Separate the language for measuring value from the governance of who handles that language and how.

## Basic Structure
**Purpose disclosure.** State what the institution exists to protect.

**Distributed authority.** Do not combine observation, evaluation, allocation, and appeal adjudication.

**Agreement memory.** Log who agreed to what and under which change conditions.

**Appeal and exit.** Provide routes for objection, pause, exit, and reconnection.

**Reversibility and remedy.** Provide procedures to correct, reallocate, and explain wrong evaluations or allocations.

**Anti-gaming.** Audit behavior that games only the metrics and revise them when needed.

**Public-safe boundary.** Show information needed for audit while separating PII and internal safety methods.

## Practical Process
### 1. State purpose and scope
Write whose what the institution protects and how.

### 2. Separate roles
Separate responsibility for observation, evaluation, allocation, and appeals, and record conflicts of interest.

### 3. Keep primary logs
Make agreements, changes, decisions, appeals, and remedies traceable later.

### 4. Make appeal and exit effective
Check not merely that a channel exists, but that it can be used without penalty.

### 5. Design reversibility
Decide how far an error can be reversed and what remedy applies to the irreversible part.

### 6. Audit gaming
Check whether metrics improved while reality worsened.

### 7. Re-audit the institution itself
Check whether current operation has drifted from its starting purpose and reach renewed agreement.

## Examples of Application
In reward systems, do not connect a single metric directly to rewards; use multiple observations, appeals, and re-evaluation. In communities, do not equate volume or popularity with authority; separate moderation reasons from appeal routes.

In AI operations, do not close model evaluation within its creators and the evaluated field; add independent observation, appeals, and model-drift checks.

## Success Criteria
- Purpose and procedures are traceable.
- Observation, evaluation, allocation, and appeal adjudication are not concentrated in one actor.
- Agreements and change history remain.
- Appeal, exit, and reconnection are actually usable.
- Errors can be corrected and remedied.
- Metric gaming can be detected and revised.
- Publicity and privacy coexist.
- The institution itself can be reevaluated.

## Limitations
Separating authority too much can blur responsibility and slow decisions. Distribution is not automatically good; state who has final responsibility.

Making every procedure public can also harm safety or privacy. Separate what should be public from what should be audited privately.

## Misreading Prevention
Governance does not mean stronger management or fine-grained surveillance of participants. It is a structure that makes authority and procedures verifiable, restores errors, and lets objections reach the system.

The minimum core is **remembering agreements without fixing authority, evaluation, and allocation into unreviewed domination**.
