## Summary
The Parent addresses governance after connection value becomes observable. Once institutions can measure, allocate, reward, or authorize on the basis of connection value, a new risk appears: the same actor may define the metric, judge performance, distribute benefits, and dismiss objections. Governance therefore protects the measurement layer from turning into a value-capture monopoly.

Agreement is institutionalized as a traceable memory and procedure, not frozen as permanent consent. Legitimate governance keeps purpose, procedure, role separation, objection, exit, reconnection, reversibility, remedy, anti-gaming controls, privacy boundaries, and institutional revision available.

## Concepts
- **Purpose publicity**: state what the institution exists to protect or allocate.
- **Procedural auditability**: preserve the path to a decision, not only the result.
- **Role separation**: avoid combining observation, evaluation, allocation, and appeal in one unchecked actor.
- **Agreement memory**: retain consent scope and change history.
- **Objection channel**: allow correction and review without punishment.
- **Exit/reconnection**: allow real departure and later return under new conditions.
- **Reversibility/remedy**: correct misallocation or misjudgment when possible.
- **Anti-gaming**: monitor divergence between metric gains and real value.
- **Public-safe boundary**: separate auditability from disclosure of PII or exploitable internal details.
- **Institutional drift**: divergence between original purpose and current operation.

## Applications
Applicable to co-creation programs, community governance, incentive systems, AI governance, inter-organizational collaboration, and public audit. The model asks who defines value, who allocates benefits, who can challenge a decision, and whether the institution can correct itself.

## Audit points
Check for self-evaluation/self-allocation/self-appeal, formal-but-useless objection channels, high exit costs, missing primary logs, absent remedies, metric gaming, privacy overexposure, leakage of sensitive internal methods, drift from original purpose, and rules that cannot be revised.

## Preconditions
Explicit purpose, role assignment, traceable primary logs, usable objection and exit, remedy, accountability, public/private information boundaries, and a revision procedure are required. Distributed authority must not erase final responsibility.

## Failure modes
Failure includes turning popularity or activity into rights, letting one actor measure and allocate to itself, punishing objections, locking participants in, changing rules without recorded reasons, treating transparency as total disclosure, or using anti-gaming language to justify arbitrary rule changes.

## Falsification / update conditions
If additional separation and review layers do not improve correction, legitimacy, or fit—and instead only create delay and responsibility diffusion—the governance stack should be simplified. Formal exit or appeal that cannot be used in practice counts as failure and requires redesign.

## Required distinctions
- governance ≠ stronger control
- institutionalized agreement ≠ permanent consent
- participation ≠ consent
- distributed authority ≠ no responsibility
- transparency ≠ total disclosure
- formal exit ≠ usable exit
- anti-gaming ≠ arbitrary change

## Misreading constraints
The Parent does not require everyone to decide everything, nor does it reject expertise. It requires expertise and authority to remain auditable, challengeable, and correctable. “Public-safe” does not mean opaque; it means auditability without indiscriminate exposure of private or exploitable details.
