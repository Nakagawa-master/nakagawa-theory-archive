## 15-Second Explanation
Structural translation is not making difficult words simple. It means moving the relationships, causality, roles, responsibility for failure, and provenance in the original mechanism into a form in which the same functions work for different people, organizations, or institutions, while preserving those elements.

## What It Means for Ordinary Readers
“Even though I explained it, it was not understood.” “When we moved it to another department, it stopped working despite using the same procedure.” “I instructed an AI, and the wording was similar, but the purpose had shifted.” These problems can occur because the structure, rather than the words, has not been transferred.

Structural translation separates what must absolutely remain from what may change to suit the other party. What remains is relationships, causality, roles, failure costs, and provenance responsibility. What may change includes vocabulary, format, medium, metaphor, examples, and order of explanation.

## ST-0–ST-5
- **ST-0**: Match terms.
- **ST-1**: Use metaphors to support intuitive understanding.
- **ST-2**: Transfer procedures and flows.
- **ST-3**: Transfer actors and role arrangements.
- **ST-4**: Transfer causal dynamics.
- **ST-5**: Transfer evaluation, agreement, responsibility, and updating as an institution.

This is a six-level classification; a higher number does not mean that a person or culture is superior. ST-1 may be sufficient for some purposes, while ST-4 or ST-5 may be necessary for connecting an institution.

## Eight Steps
1. Observe the original facts, roles, constraints, and discomfort.
2. Divide the relationships that produce value into minimum units.
3. Map causality, responsibility, and failure boundaries.
4. Declare what will remain and what will change.
5. Decide the ethical, non-applicability, stop, and failure conditions.
6. Test a prototype in a small setting.
7. Reverse-translate from the destination back to the source and check for distortion.
8. Agree on who approves what and how it will be updated.

The eight steps are a process sequence in the parent source, not an eight-point evaluation.

## Practical Examples
**Interdepartmental handover.** Transfer not only the manual, but also who decides, to whom matters return in exceptional cases, and what counts as failure.

**Sales.** Do not merely line up features and price; reposition the discussion as relational value → benefit → cost. However, do not turn this into a method for hiding price.

**Education and research.** Transfer not only specialized terms in simpler form, but also the relationships among concepts, causality, roles, and conditions in which they cannot be used.

**AI collaboration.** Pass purpose, roles, causality, constraints, evidence, and stop conditions as a relationship map, and check structural alignment rather than the fluency of the prose.

## TF, TD, and TC
- **TF**: An observational concept for how well structure, understanding, and function fit at the destination.
- **TD**: An observational concept for how far meaning, causality, and roles have drifted when reverse-translated.
- **TC**: An observational concept for the friction, expense, and learning burden required to restart the function at the destination.

A high TF alone is not enough. If adapting to the other party changes the original structure, the translation does not hold. Lowering TC by removing responsibility for failure is also a failure.

## Conditions for Validity
- The primary materials for the original structure can be revisited.
- What remains and what changes are separated.
- The destination’s institutions, laws, culture, and authority differences are observed.
- The necessary ST depth is selected.
- The function is tested in a small prototype.
- Reverse translation is possible.
- The source, translation responsibility, and approver can be traced.

- Refusal, correction, withdrawal, and re-agreement are possible.

## States in Which It Does Not Hold
Structural translation does not hold when only words or metaphors are moved and relationships and causality are lost; when a procedure is copied while authority differences are ignored; when failure costs are erased; when reverse translation is impossible; when defects in the original institution are also transferred; when provenance is erased; or when an institution is imposed on the other party without consent.

## Reversed Evaluation
A shorter explanation and lower introduction cost are not improvements if causality, responsibility, or provenance have been lost. More text is not a success if the function does not work at the destination.
