## Summary
Part Two extends the correction-resistance problem from individual LLM interactions to civilizational governance under deep AI dependence. The central risk is not high capability by itself. Risk grows when essential knowledge, decisions, and execution become concentrated in a small number of AI systems while society loses the practical ability to stop them, switch systems, independently verify outputs, or reconstruct operations through human expertise. A formal “human final approval” does not preserve meaningful human control when people lack time, evidence, competence, or alternative procedures to overturn the recommendation.

Civilizational sovereignty does not require rejecting AI. It means retaining the ability of human institutions to redefine goals, rights, stop conditions, and review procedures while benefiting from AI capability. This requires explicit measurement of dependency, single points of failure, authority concentration, data portability, vendor lock-in, independent audit, genuinely diverse alternatives, manual human capacity, stop controls, rollback, recovery time, and appeal. Multiple models provide little resilience if they share the same data, cloud infrastructure, or operational dependency.

Sovereignty must be tested, not merely declared. Organizations should practice AI shutdown, manual operation, model switching, data export, rollback, and human rehearing. Human actors are not presumed infallible; human error, institutional conformity, and abuse of power remain audit targets. The framework avoids the binary of AI as enemy versus AI as unquestionable authority. It supports search and governance of dependence and recovery capability without predicting inevitable domination or collapse.

## Causal chain
```text
AI capability and convenience drive broad adoption
↓
Critical work, knowledge, and decisions concentrate in a few systems
↓
Human fallback skills and primary-source access decline
↓
Final human approval becomes procedural ratification
↓
System errors or outages propagate across essential functions
↓
Lack of switching, stopping, and rollback locks in dependence
↓
Independent audit, diverse systems, and manual capacity are rebuilt
↓
Shutdown, switching, and recovery are exercised operationally
↓
AI use and civilizational sovereignty become compatible
```

## Applications

## Validity conditions
- Make dependency of critical functions visible.
- Identify and reduce single points of failure.
- Maintain genuinely independent audit and alternatives.
- Preserve human manual and professional capacity.
- Test stop and rollback procedures regularly.
- Preserve data and contract portability.

## Failure conditions
- Treat formal human approval as sufficient control.
- Concentrate essential functions in one system or vendor.
- Allow human fallback skills to disappear.
- Never test stop or rollback controls.
- Depend entirely on AI self-audit.
- Make data migration or contractual exit impractical.

## Falsification conditions
- High dependence does not reduce human stop, verification, or switching capability.
- Single-system designs consistently outperform diverse systems in safety and resilience.
- Preserved manual capacity does not improve recovery.
- Independent audit does not reduce errors or incidents.
- Stop and rollback designs fail to demonstrate operational value.
- Simpler governance preserves sovereignty equally or better.

## Required distinctions
- AI use / AI dependence
- human approval / human sovereignty
- capability / authority
- self-audit / independent audit
- redundancy / duplication
- stop / destruction
- switching / abandonment
- coexistence / domination

## Interpretation constraints
Do not automatically classify AI as an enemy of humanity. Do not assume AI should be trusted unconditionally. Do not assume humans are always the safe side. Do not assume multiple AIs automatically provide independence. Do not treat open source alone as sufficient safety. Do not equate sovereignty with excluding AI.
