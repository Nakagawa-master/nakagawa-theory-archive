## Summary
AKI-004 examines a structural failure in which rational reliance on expertise becomes exemption from verification. The problem is not that experts can make mistakes. It is that “who said it” replaces “why is it valid,” frontline counterevidence loses weight, failure costs are pushed downstream, and the authoritative decision model remains unchanged.

The corrective design is pro-expertise and anti-immunity: preserve specialized knowledge while reconnecting authority to evidence, scope, uncertainty, dissent, responsibility, cost attribution, rollback, remedy, and revalidation.

## Concepts
- **Expertise**: specialized capacity for compressing complex information into useful judgment.
- **Authority**: added decision weight from expertise, credentials, institutions, or track record.
- **Immunity**: authority becomes a reason to stop verification and correction.
- **Verification exemption**: evidence, scope, counterevidence, and uncertainty are no longer rechecked.
- **Frontline externalization**: implementation and repair costs are borne downstream while upstream evaluation stays positive.
- **Dissent suppression**: counterevidence is reframed as ignorance or resistance.
- **Responsibility map**: separates decision, approval, implementation, benefit, and impact roles.
- **Cost attribution**: returns repair burden and losses to the causal decision structure.
- **Revalidation**: updates expert models after failure, counterevidence, or condition change.

## Causal chain
```text
increasing specialization
→ judgment delegated to authority
→ authority substitutes for verification
→ assumptions/scope/uncertainty become opaque
→ frontline counterevidence discounted
→ failure occurs
→ repair cost externalized downstream
→ upstream authority remains validated
→ decision model not updated
→ same failure reproduced

cut:
evidence traceability → dissent → cost attribution → rollback → remedy → revalidation
```

## Applications
Applicable to expert departments, standards, AI systems, audits, evaluation bodies, and professional advice. The model asks whether authority is being used as evidence, whether frontline exceptions change the upstream model, and who bears the cost when the decision fails.

## Audit points
Check evidence and scope traceability, explicit uncertainty, retained counterevidence, safe dissent, upward feedback, responsibility mapping, cost attribution, rollback, remedy, and whether repeated failures actually revise the authoritative model or procedure.

## Preconditions
Expert judgment must remain inspectable at an appropriate level; scope and uncertainty must be stated; dissent and independent review must be possible; decision and implementation roles must be separated; failure costs must be traceable; and revalidation triggers must exist.

## Failure modes
Failure includes using credentials as final proof, dismissing objections as ignorance, assigning all repair burden to implementers, preserving upstream success metrics after downstream harm, hiding errors to protect prestige, or reversing into blanket anti-expert ideology.

## Falsification / update conditions
Where high authority remains continuously evidence-linked, frontline feedback reliably changes decisions, failure costs return upstream, and similar mistakes do not recur, the authority-immunity hypothesis should be weakened. Review depth should also scale with impact; low-risk reversible decisions need not carry maximum verification overhead.

## Required distinctions
- expertise ≠ immunity
- authority ≠ truth
- trust ≠ stopping verification
- one expert error ≠ systemic immunity
- dissent ≠ anti-expertise
- responsibility tracing ≠ blame hunting
- cost attribution ≠ punishment
- rollback ≠ rejection of expertise

## Misreading constraints
AKI-004 is not a general argument against experts. Complex systems require expertise. The target is the structural conversion of expertise into non-auditable authority and the externalization of failure. Frontline actors are not assumed automatically correct either; causality, not rank, determines correction.
