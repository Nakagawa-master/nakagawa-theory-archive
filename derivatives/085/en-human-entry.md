## 15-Second Explanation
The parent original positions the question “Is there funding?” as a shallow D1 question for considering institutional sustainability. The necessary move is to advance to D3, asking which causal structure should be changed, and to D4, asking how to design an institution that can function even with an inverted population pyramid; then find the KGI and core-point KPI and redesign the institution itself.

## Why It Becomes Necessary
The parent original argues that Japanese political debate has long been trapped in the question “Is there funding now?” As a result, tax rates, burdens, and changes in benefits may be debated, while the upstream question of whether the institution’s premises themselves fit changes in population structure has remained weak. That is the problem setting.

The original organizes D1 as “Is there funding now?”, D3 as “Which causal structure must change for the institution to be sustainable?”, and D4 as “Which institutional design should be introduced so that it functions even with an inverted pyramid?” D1, D3, and D4 are labels for question depth, not scores.

It also depicts the reason shallow questions continue as a three-way structure of politicians, media, and the public. The focus here is not to blame the three parties, but to identify the structure in which each party’s behavior reproduces the shallow question “Where is the funding?”

## Practical Process
**1. Check whether you have stopped at D1.** Do not reach a conclusion based only on “How much is available now?”

**2. Move to D3.** Ask which causal structure must change for the institution to become sustainable.

**3. Move to D4.** Design a combination of institutions that can function even with an inverted population pyramid.

**4. Set the KGI.** Specify the state to be achieved, such as the “establishment of sustainable social security and taxation” indicated by the parent original.

**5. Search for the core-point KPI.** Examine through causality candidate fulcrums that act on the KGI, such as a structural transformation of elderly medical-cost burdens, an institutional extension of the working-participation age, and a structural replacement of asset and consumption taxation.

**6. Keep it falsifiable.** Do not fix an institutional proposal as a political answer; keep it open to revision through audit results, consent, reversibility, and renewed agreement.

## Applications
**1. Social security.** Compare designs in which benefits, burdens, and participation remain sustainable even when population structure changes, rather than asking only “How much additional funding is needed?”

**2. Elderly medical care.** Examine a change in the burden structure as a candidate core point connected to the KGI, not merely as an increase or decrease in burdens.

**3. Labor participation.** Read an institutional extension of the working-participation age as a candidate causal fulcrum for population structure and institutional sustainability.

**4. Taxation.** Compare a structural replacement of asset and consumption taxation as a candidate institutional design, rather than on a single increase-tax / cut-tax axis.

## Success Criteria
Do not stop after answering the question “Is there funding?”; be able to explain the causality of why the institution is or is not sustainable.

Deepen the question through D3 and D4, and maintain a verifiable causal line between the institutional proposal and the KGI.

Do not fix core-point KPI candidates as policy slogans; revise them if results do not appear.

Even when decisions are fast, do not lose dissent, renewed agreement, or reversibility through volume bias or fixing an early winner.

## Limitations
Expressions such as “30 years,” “more than 30 years,” and “the 1980s” in the parent original position the author’s historical argument. They must be treated as the original’s line of reasoning, not reread as independently verified statistical conclusions.

The elderly medical care, labor participation, and asset/consumption taxation described by the parent original are examples of core-point candidates; they are not to be fixed as the only policy answer requiring no verification.

## Preventing Misreading
This is not a theory that says one must not ask “Where is the funding?” The problem is closing institutional design with only the funding question.

Do not replace it with the simplistic opposite claim that funding is unnecessary. The focus is upstream design that includes real-material constraints, institutional operation, and the relationship between burdens and benefits.

Do not turn the three-way structure of politicians, media, and the public into personal attacks or partisan blame. The parent original addresses the structure that reproduces shallow questions.
