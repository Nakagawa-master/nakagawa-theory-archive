## Summary
Meaning Convergence Theory does not claim that a future event physically rewrites the past. It places a **feasible future definition**, checked against real constraints, as a terminal meaning condition and then asks how unchanged past and present nodes—experience, skills, relationships, resources, failures, outcomes, and unfinished work—should be reinterpreted relative to the conditions required for that future. The result may be a change in meaning vectors, node connections, priorities, and present decisions. The theory is valid only if this semantic rearrangement reaches actual action and remains revisable when implementation evidence contradicts the future definition.

## Concepts
- future definition: a describable, testable, revisable future state; not prophecy or fate.
- feasibility: the audit of whether the future definition can exist under resource, time, institutional, capability, and external constraints.
- node: a unit such as experience, skill, relationship, resource, failure, achievement, or unfinished work whose role can be reevaluated.
- meaning vector: a conceptual direction representing how a node functions relative to a future definition—as resource, learning, obstacle, redundancy, or another role.
- backward comparison: comparing present and past nodes against future requirements; not physical retrocausality.
- semantic rearrangement: changing the role or priority assigned to unchanged facts.
- connection redesign: linking nodes differently when a future definition reveals a previously unused relation among them.
- priority update: changing continue/stop/withdraw/invest/learn decisions after semantic reevaluation.
- action alignment: translating semantic change into concrete present decisions.
- falsification and redefinition: revising the future definition or node arrangement when reality does not support them.

## Causal chain
present-only evaluation → past meanings become fixed → failure is treated as waste, detour as loss, and current resources only by current use → define a feasible future state → audit constraints → extract conditions required for that future → compare those conditions back to past and present nodes → reclassify nodes as resources, learning, obstacles, or unnecessary elements → redesign node connections → update priorities for continuation, stopping, withdrawal, investment, and learning → align present action → observe implementation → revise the future definition or semantic arrangement when evidence contradicts it.

## State model
- local-present evaluation: nodes are valued only through past labels or current use.
- fixed-meaning phase: prior interpretation remains detached from future conditions.
- future-definition phase: a future state is expressed as a revisable condition.
- feasibility-audit phase: the future is checked against real constraints.
- backward-comparison phase: each node is examined against future requirements.
- semantic-rearrangement phase: meaning vectors change without changing the facts themselves.
- reconnection phase: node-to-node and node-to-future links are redesigned.
- priority-update phase: continue, stop, withdraw, invest, or learn decisions change.
- action-alignment phase: semantic changes reach actual present behavior.
- revalidation phase: implementation evidence updates the future definition and node interpretation.

## Applications
In career design, multiple roles that once looked unrelated can be tested as a skill bundle required by a future role, while genuinely irrelevant experience can remain irrelevant rather than being romanticized. In business, a failed project may contain specific customer knowledge or operational assets that become useful in a new future configuration without turning the entire failure into a success story. In research, a future verifiable state can change which experiments or learning activities deserve priority now. In institutional design, inherited components can be separated into future resources and future obstacles. In every case, reinterpretation must alter present decisions rather than merely produce a more comforting narrative.

## Measurements and audit
Audit whether the future definition is specific; whether real constraints are explicit; whether each node’s old and new role can be explained; whether the reason for semantic change traces to a future requirement; whether connections among nodes actually change; whether priorities or withdrawal/investment decisions change; whether present behavior differs; whether inconvenient evidence remains visible; whether alternative future definitions can be compared; and whether implementation can force revision. Do not invent numeric meaning vectors, fixed thresholds, realization probabilities, or dates absent from the Parent.

## Validity conditions
The theory is useful when the future definition is connected to real constraints, backward comparison produces explainable changes in node roles, connections, and priorities, those changes produce actual differences in present action, and implementation results can revise the future definition or semantic arrangement. Merely declaring a future goal or feeling differently about the past is insufficient.

## Failure conditions
The theory fails when converted into prophecy, fate, wish fulfillment, physical retrocausality, selective evidence deletion, or post-hoc success storytelling in which every prior failure is declared necessary. It also fails when an infeasible future remains fixed, when changed meaning creates no decision difference, or when the theory is used to erase adverse facts rather than preserve them.

## Falsification conditions
Revise or narrow the theory when the future definition is infeasible under real constraints, semantic rearrangement does not change priorities or behavior, another future definition persistently explains the same nodes better, or the same reevaluation is fully explained without using a future definition. Falsification is not external to the theory; it is the mechanism by which future definitions and meaning arrangements remain non-dogmatic.

## Required distinctions
future definition / future prophecy; changed meaning / changed fact; feasibility / inevitability; backward comparison / physical retrocausality; meaning convergence / outcome bias or post-hoc rationalization; terminal meaning condition / fate; reevaluation / romanticization of the past; action alignment / interpretation-only change.

## Interpretation constraints
Do not generalize the theory into quantum retrocausality, destiny, attraction doctrines, or confirmed information arriving from the future. Do not delete inconvenient evidence. Do not retain only facts that support the chosen future. Do not add unsupported realization probabilities, numeric meaning vectors, or target dates. Keep the future definition revisable and require semantic change to connect to observable present decisions and later revalidation.
