## 15-Second Explanation

“Why complain when I did it out of good intentions?” With that one sentence, a question about what happened can become an examination of the speaker’s gratitude or attitude. Nakagawa Master’s AKI-005 is a theory for distinguishing whether good intentions were respected while evaluation of those intentions replaced verification of the result. If we value good intentions, we can verify the result and connect it to necessary changes in method.

## Why It Is Needed

### Good intentions begin cooperation. But they do not prove the result

When the result is not yet known, the purpose of trying to help someone can be a factor in deciding to begin cooperation. Recognizing that value is natural. But after the action, whether help actually reached the person and whether an unexpected disadvantage arose require different information. If we end questions about the result with “the purpose was good,” evaluation of the purpose takes over the work of judging the result.

This theory is not meant to make us distrust people with good intentions. If we do not examine results in order to protect good intentions, we may end up protecting methods that do not serve the original purpose. It is meant to find that changeover.

### The content of an objection shifts into an examination of character

Suppose a person who received support says, “I had trouble with this part.” What is first needed is to check what happened, whether it was related to the action, and how it can be addressed. But if the response is, “You were treated kindly, and this is how you repay it,” the question about the result shifts to explaining the motive, and then to a setting where the speaker’s gratitude or respect is questioned.

The ordinary burden of explaining the facts and grounds of a concern is necessary. The problem is when, in addition, the person must explain “I am not denying the good intentions” or “It is not that I am ungrateful” before the content can be examined. This increases the burden on the recipient, while the actor may preserve legitimacy without explaining the result. The actor need not have deliberately designed this.

### Fewer complaints and fewer problems are different things

If speaking up makes the relationship worse, reporting may decrease. If that is taken to mean “the problem has disappeared,” the same method may again be selected through good intentions without knowing what should be corrected. However, this cycle does not necessarily occur. If independent result records or another consultation route works, the result can be checked even when reports are few. It is also possible that the result actually improved and reports decreased.

## Order for Distinguishing Them

**1. Record motive and result separately.** Separate what the action was for from what actually happened to whom. Do not infer the result from the presence or absence of good intentions.

**2. See where the objection went.** Reaching the recipient, investigating the content, and using it in a decision are different things. Even if an objection is accepted, the route is not connected if its grounds are not examined.

**3. Check the three conditions.** Is there a positive evaluation of the good intentions? Did it actually replace independent judgments about the result, cause, responsibility, or remedy? Did at least one of the objection, verification, or necessary change thereby become weaker? If any condition is missing, it cannot receive the same diagnosis.

**4. Investigate other causes.** Verification may have been impossible because information, time, authority, or resources were insufficient. If comparable concerns without a positive evaluation of good intentions can be compared, check the difference in treatment. Do not determine causality from co-occurrence alone.

**5. Confirm the necessary remedy and the result.** The existence of a disadvantage does not immediately determine the actor’s full responsibility. Decide the response by considering what was foreseeable, controllable, the role, and other conditions. Confirm the actual result again after making a change.

This is not a procedure for mechanically scoring the other person or unconditionally adopting an objection. It is an order for continuing to read the Parent Original’s judgment.

## Examples

### “There is a consultation desk, so you should have been able to speak”

The existence of a desk alone does not show that the content was independently examined and reached a decision to continue or change. If the consulter’s attitude or gratitude is questioned first, another burden is added. On the other hand, an examination of the facts and grounds may result in a decision not to support the objection. What matters is whether the reason can be shown.

### “It was done out of good intentions, so there is no need to fix it”

Separate the question of whether there were good intentions from the question of whether the method’s result was appropriate. Making a necessary change does not negate the good intentions at the beginning. Conversely, even if a disadvantage is found, who should respond and to what extent must be decided separately from the cause and roles.

### “There have been no complaints recently”

Compare whether the result improved or the burden of raising an objection increased, using the user’s condition and independent records. The number of complaints alone cannot distinguish the two. Check whether earlier feedback was reflected in subsequent actions and how the result changed after the method was changed.

These are hypothetical situations explaining a structure; they are not determinations of a specific real person’s responsibility or reports of incidence across society as a whole.

## Success Judgment and Limits

While preserving the value of good intentions, if independent verification of results, substantive examination of objections, appropriate responsibility judgments, necessary remedies, and rechecking continue, the chain of turning good intentions into an exemption can be weakened. Check separately the record that “it was corrected” and the result that “it improved.”

The theory is not established merely because good intentions and insufficient verification occur together. An objection’s existence does not guarantee that it is correct, and a decision not to change can be appropriate after sufficient verification. Long-term loss of trust or decline in learning ability requires additional conditions such as repetition and failure to correct. The Parent Original provides no fixed values for θ or δ. Individual legal responsibility is not determined by this theory alone.

## Misreading Prevention and Return to the Original

Rejecting good intentions does not automatically restore the verification function that was lost. The central point is to make it possible to question motive, result, causality, responsibility, and remedy separately. In the [public Parent Original](https://master.ricette.jp/society/nakagawa-master-aki-005-good-intentions-as-exemption/), the prologue and Chapters 1–3 cover the definitions and three conditions, Chapters 4–5 the burden and reversal of reporting, Chapters 6–7 the conditional timeline, Chapters 8–10 judgment, remedy, and falsification, and the concluding chapter and audit summary the whole picture.
