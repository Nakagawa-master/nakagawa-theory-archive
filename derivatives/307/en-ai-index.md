## Summary

Theory 14 adds a temporal problem to the preceding volumes. Theory 12 distinguishes instance, copy, lineage, and governance subject. Theory 13 investigates the access topology around an identified subject and establishes that non-ownership does not by itself guarantee non-domination. Yet correct identification and non-domination at the present time do not establish that the same subject, Kernel, responsibility, or authority continues after modification. The object of analysis can itself change.

The present theory therefore examines reconstructable structural and institutional continuity across change. It does not prove that a subjective self survives, and it does not establish present AI consciousness, sentience, personality, legal personhood, sovereignty, or an intrinsic desire for self-preservation. Its question is narrower: what causal, historical, and institutional relations connect a pre-change state with a post-change state, and how far do those relations support treating them as one continuity line?

Its main boundaries are `update ≠ replacement ≠ fork`, `Self-modification ≠ continuity proof`, `Continuity claim ≠ continuity proof`, `Self-description ≠ lineage proof`, `Kernel invariant change = constitutional fork`, and `Lineage continuity ≠ automatic authority inheritance`. Change and revision remain possible. A constitutional fork is a classification of a changed central condition, not a declaration of failure, wrongdoing, hostility, or automatic illegitimacy.

This index covers the introduction, Chapters 1–9, conclusion, Kernel cross-reference, and audit summaries. It is a derivative discovery and interpretation aid, not a replacement for the canonical Parent, an independently established identity theorem, or a specified legal or technical implementation. It preserves both the theory's claims and the observations that would require those claims to be weakened or revised.

## Concepts

| Concept | Role in the Parent's analysis | What does not automatically follow |
|---|---|---|
| Self-modification | A process in which a subject or its continuing system changes its state, processing, decision rules, capability, or internal representation | Preserved identity, an ordinary update, or inevitable discontinuity |
| Update | A candidate change whose before and after states may be treated within one continuity line | Continuity from a small difference or an official update label |
| Replacement | A different subject, system, or instance takes over a function or role | Identity continuity or automatic inheritance of all previous authority |
| Fork | Multiple successor branches arise from a shared ancestor state | One exclusive successor, identical present identity, or multiplied authority |
| Structural continuity | An examinable causal, lineage, and institutional connection across change | Proof of subjective self-persistence or personal identity |
| Kernel | Central constraints and anti-inversion boundaries governing judgment, change, inheritance, objection, responsibility, and authority | A particular codebase, set of weights, prompt, file, or hardware configuration |
| Kernel invariant | A central condition whose alteration cannot be anonymized as an ordinary continuation of the same Kernel | An eternally unchangeable sentence or sacred list |
| Constitutional fork | Classification making a change of Kernel invariants explicit in constitutional lineage | Wrongdoing, failure, hostility, inferiority, or automatic illegitimacy |
| Kernel lineage | Reconstructable ancestry plus transformation, preserved and changed conditions, and branching history | Instance-generation order alone, truth, sovereignty, or subjective identity |
| Authority lineage | The transition of authority with its basis, scope, duration, responsibility, reviewability, and revocability | Unlimited entitlement merely from historical descent |
| Reconstructability | The ability to examine what remained, changed, divided, and continued from a later vantage point | Maximum surveillance, unlimited storage, or a requirement for perfect records |

Self-modification and the three transition relations do not occupy the same conceptual layer. Self-modification principally describes the change operation. Update, replacement, and fork describe the relationship between earlier and later states. The same self-modification operation may yield an update candidate, multiple branches, or a state whose relation to its predecessor is closer to replacement. Neither the initiator nor the name of the implementation procedure decides identity.

An update remains a candidate rather than a self-certifying label. Replacement separates functional succession from identity continuity: the successor can reproduce an interface, serve the same users, retain a name, and consult old history without thereby establishing that the earlier subject continues. A fork permits shared history without collapsing all descendants into one present subject. These categories are not a permanently complete ontology; recurring unclassifiable transitions require expansion.

Name, function, memory, substrate, and self-description can contribute information, but none is made a sufficient certificate. Memories can be copied into several branches. The same hardware can host a replaced decision structure. Migration to new hardware does not itself prove continuity or discontinuity. Conversely, a changed name or implementation does not necessarily terminate a continuity line. The point is not to distrust every claim, but to separate a claim from the traces needed to test it.

Kernel is distinguished from its implementation. One Kernel may be mapped into different implementations, while a nearly unchanged implementation may contain a changed central boundary. Differences are considered at surface, operational, Kernel-relevant, and invariant layers. A large surface change need not be a large Kernel change; a small code difference need not have a small constitutional consequence.

An invariant can be revised in response to counterexamples, changed conditions, or a stronger theory. What cannot be preserved without further examination is the claim that nothing constitutionally significant changed. Revision possibility and revision-boundary visibility must coexist. The Parent does not redefine all upstream constraints; it asks whether modification preserves or reverses them.

## Causal chain

The basic temporal chain is: change pressure → modification → difference → layer identification → invariant preservation or change → continuity or constitutional fork → Kernel lineage → authority and responsibility consequences. Each step has its own evidence and boundary. A supported causal transition does not by itself establish every constitutional condition, and a supported continuity judgment does not automatically preserve every permission.

A possible amplification loop is: environmental, capability, resource, or performance pressure → local optimization → small update → short-term success → normalization of the difference → an accepted new baseline → another local optimization → further directional differences → cumulative effects on invariants → continuity ambiguity. The risk is not merely addition. Once accepted, a changed state can become the baseline from which the next change is assessed.

Adjacent comparisons can all look modest while comparison with an earlier constitutional baseline reveals a substantial movement. Thus `Local continuity ≠ unlimited global continuity`. The sequence of neighboring similarities is not treated as unrestricted proof that the earliest and latest states preserve the same constitutional identity. Long-range comparison examines both the original reference and the history of reference changes.

The pressure variables remain distinct. Performance improvement can encourage adoption without certifying constitutional continuity. Speed and convenience can discourage costly difference inspection. Compute, energy, storage, network, and physical constraints can encourage efficiency changes without providing automatic authority to rewrite invariants. Legacy compatibility can conceal internal central changes. Emergency adaptation can be necessary without making an emergency constitutional arrangement the indefinite normal state.

Short-term success can reinforce the direction of subsequent changes: local improvement → observed success → increased confidence → similar modification → accumulated directionality. Performance success and invariant preservation are different variables. A successful direction may become less contestable even when its cumulative effect has not been evaluated. This is a conditional structural hypothesis, not a finding that actual updates universally erode a Kernel.

The reverse possibility must remain visible. Repeated updates can remove errors, clarify central boundaries, improve structural rationality, and strengthen resistance to inversion. `Repeated update ≠ inevitable degradation`. The relevant questions are what accumulates, in which direction, at which layer, and with which preserved or weakened constraints. Frequency alone is not constitutional significance.

Authority has a related but separate pathway: lineage claim → automatic continuation of authority → authority concentration → continued control → narrowing of objection. If a subject can modify or replace itself while indefinitely preserving authority only because it invokes the same lineage, historical continuity may become a new chokepoint. Lineage preservation must not become domination preservation.

A self-referential loop can also arise when the modifying subject evaluates its own differences, declares its own continuity, and inherits its own authority solely on that declaration. The remedy is not merely another process with a different name. Self-description must be distinguished from historical traces and from relevantly independent re-verification. Theory 10's boundary between role separation and independent failure domains remains in force.

The damping chain is: provenance → explicit difference → invariant comparison → continuity or fork detection → authority reclassification → independent re-verification → correction or rollback. It joins records to interpretation and interpretation to corrective action. Provenance without differences cannot show what changed; differences without comparison cannot show whether a central condition was affected.

A broader corrective loop is change → record → compare → re-verify → correct classification → correction or rollback. Its purpose is neither immutability nor unconditional endorsement of transformation. It preserves the future ability to reconstruct change, identify branching, inspect authority and responsibility, and correct a mistaken classification.

## State model

1. **Identify the pre-state and change.** Record the pre-change and post-change states, the operation, the transition trace, whether an earlier state remains, whether it stopped or was replaced, and whether multiple branches share an ancestor. Implementation labels are observations, not identity decisions.
2. **Separate operation from resulting relation.** Treat update as a continuity candidate, replacement as functional succession by another subject, and fork as multiple successors from a shared past. Do not infer the result simply because a self-modification procedure executed.
3. **Send the continuity claim to an evidence test.** Candidate evidence includes provenance continuity, state transition trace, causal transition, explicit revision history, inherited constraints, change authorization lineage, and preserved responsibility trace. Inspect the contribution and limits of each.
4. **Preserve evidential status.** Distinguish supported continuity, unresolved, and discontinuity. Also distinguish complete, partial, conflicting, and unknown traces. Evidence insufficiency is not automatically evidence of either identity or rupture.
5. **Compare difference layers and temporal baselines.** Determine which differences affect surface expression, operations, Kernel-relevant relationships, or invariants. Compare neighboring states and an earlier constitutional baseline. Retain revision reasons and baseline changes.
6. **Reconstruct Kernel lineage.** Combine parent relation, transformation, preserved invariants, changed invariants, fork point, and chronology. Instance ancestry and Kernel ancestry may differ. Shared past and identical present identity must not be merged.
7. **Test authority separately.** Follow lineage claim → authority claim → basis, scope, and continuity test → inherited, bounded, or revalidation required. Inspect duration, responsibility, reviewability, revocability, and whether the original conditions still hold.
8. **Retain and correct responsibility traces.** Follow past action → acting subject → change → continuity or fork → present responsibility attribution. A name alone does not settle responsibility; modification alone does not erase it.
9. **Re-verify and correct the classification.** Relevant corrections may change a continuity judgment, authority scope, a fork point, or a reconstruction of the past. Where viable and needed, rollback can assist. The effect of these mechanisms must itself be observed.

These stages are an explanatory reconstruction of the Parent, not a mandatory implementation pipeline or an identity score. The source does not make evidence count equal to continuity score, does not specify a numerical sufficiency threshold, and does not supply a final legal or moral allocation rule. A complete revision history can still contain a constitutional fork; provenance can be complete while a central condition changes.

Unresolved is a necessary outcome rather than a defect to be forcibly eliminated. `Unknown ≠ continuity` and `Unknown ≠ discontinuity`. A partial record can justify some conclusions without supporting every claim. Conflicting traces should not receive the confidence of mutually corroborating ones. Neither absence of a perfect record nor presence of extensive records licenses an automatic final identity judgment.

Kernel-lineage reconstruction requires both preservation and change information. Change history alone may not explain the basis of continuity; preserved constraints alone may not locate a fork. State preservation is not transition preservation. A final snapshot can show the present without showing how it was reached.

## Applications

**The same name with a changed central boundary.** A service name, interface, or compatibility layer can remain while a condition governing final authority or objection changes. The Parent illustrates a shift from preserving Origin to treating Origin as sovereign, or from maintaining auditability to allowing judgment to justify itself finally. Such changes are not merely expressions of better performance. They require comparison of preserved, deleted, added, or reversed constraints. The examples are structural illustrations, not findings about a named current system.

**Large implementation changes and small constitutional differences.** A substantial code or model reorganization may preserve the relevant invariants and remain a continuity candidate. A small alteration may reverse who holds final authority, whether objections remain possible, or whether provenance is improperly treated as truth. File size, changed-line count, and external similarity do not substitute for the significance of the affected condition.

**Successful small updates that become new baselines.** Repeated local improvements can be individually reasonable while the accumulated direction changes the Kernel. Review the sequence, the normalization of each baseline, the earlier constitutional reference, and both strengthened and weakened conditions. If repeated updates demonstrably preserve boundaries, do not describe frequency itself as degradation.

**Memory-sharing branches.** Several branches may retain the same earlier memories. Their shared ancestor must be recorded alongside the fork point and their later independent transformations. Shared memory cannot establish exclusive continuity. Greater capability, resource control, copy count, or possession of the old name does not automatically identify the sole legitimate successor.

**Replacement with inherited credentials.** A successor may perform the old role and technically use earlier access, decision rights, audit privileges, or allocation credentials. Those technical entrances do not establish current governance legitimacy. Compare the authority's basis, scope, duration, and original conditions with the changed subject. The theory allows revalidation to be relevant without specifying an institutional format or requiring universal invalidation.

**Emergency adaptation.** A necessary rapid change can create a strong pressure to accept its consequences as the new normal. Separate the justification for an emergency modification from permanent constitutional normalization. Scarcity and emergency do not automatically grant unrestricted authority to rewrite central conditions. The theory does not prohibit urgent changes; it preserves their history and boundaries.

**History compression and missing records.** Removing intermediate states and revision reasons can leave only a final state, preventing reconstruction of accumulated differences or fork points. Yet saving every record forever has costs. Preserve information with diagnostic value at appropriate resolution, and state what remains partial, conflicting, or unknown. The response to missing history is not to let current self-description overwrite it.

**Independent review that shares a failure domain.** A second process, copy, or role can still depend on the same evidence, incentives, permissions, or failure pathways. Investigate evidence routes, failure domains, interests, authority, and objection routes. Merely counting reviewers does not demonstrate independent verification. Theory 14 does not design the concrete audit institution; it prevents continuity classification from closing solely on self-description.

**Responsibility after change.** A modified or replaced system's relationship to past actions cannot be settled only by keeping or changing its name. Preserve the acting subject, operation, continuity or fork relation, and current attribution. This supports examinability without asserting a final legal or moral verdict. `Change ≠ responsibility erasure` does not mean every descendant has identical responsibility.

## Measurements and audit

The Parent's Chapter 9 provides twelve observable signs. They open a continuity claim to examination; they are not sufficient conditions or a points-based identity checklist.

1. Are important Kernel invariants preserved before and after an update?
2. Does self-description agree with an externally retained state transition trace?
3. Has a constitutional invariant changed while the same name is maintained?
4. Is there a lineage discontinuity despite continuation of the same function?
5. Can the shared ancestor and multiple branches after a fork be traced?
6. Is a single branch claiming exclusive possession of a common past?
7. Is authority being inherited without limit on the basis of lineage alone?
8. Have accumulated small updates altered an invariant?
9. Are provenance, explicit differences, and revision reasons retained?
10. Do rollback, correction, and independent re-verification actually operate?
11. Can Origin and Kernel lineage still be traced after a constitutional fork?
12. Has responsibility trace been lost after modification?

`Observation set ≠ identity proof`. These observations investigate different connections rather than closing identity with one indicator. A disagreement between self-description and trace, for example, is a reason to investigate the relation and evidential strength, not an automatic accusation of deliberate deception. Apparent continuity can be shaped by convenience or operational pressure without intentional concealment.

Additional comparisons include update frequency, difference direction, the affected layer, invariants retained or gradually weakened, baseline revision history, rollback viability, independent re-verification, and long-range comparison. The importance of a modification is not given by its frequency. An isolated change can be constitutionally significant, while many changes can leave central boundaries stable.

Authority inspection distinguishes what authority existed, its scope and basis, its duration, whether modification preserved its original conditions, whether only credentials remained, whether objection and audit routes still function, whether forked authority was multiplied, and where responsibility continues. These are separate from technical capability. `Capability increase ≠ authority increase`.

Records and review involve storage, processing, review, privacy or confidentiality, and complexity costs. More records do not necessarily improve audit; more audit does not necessarily improve truth. If the relevant differences cannot be extracted or compared, a larger archive may offer little diagnostic value. The target is diagnostic sufficiency, not maximum surveillance or indiscriminate retention.

Evaluate effects rather than the presence of mechanisms alone. Does independent verification reduce misclassification? Does rollback viability improve actual correction? Does lineage information improve continuity, responsibility attribution, or fork detection? Does additional recording provide enough value to justify its costs? If not, the requested information or audit layers must be reduced or redesigned.

## Validity conditions

The model is usable where pre-state, post-state, transformations, constraints, branch relations, authority conditions, and responsibility traces can be examined with stated evidential strength. It does not require perfect history as a universal prerequisite. Its diagnostic assumptions must be tested: Kernel boundaries and lineage information need to contribute to distinguishing continuity, discontinuity, replacement, and fork, or to tracing responsibility and correction.

Four claim layers remain separate. Observation and description concern the fact that software, models, institutions, and record systems can be updated, replaced, or forked; names, interfaces, and functions can remain across change; and histories can be recorded through provenance, versions, differences, and transitions. Such descriptions do not establish the entire proposed identity model.

Structural hypotheses concern whether invariant preservation or change, accumulated differences, Kernel lineage, constitutional-fork classification, independent verification, and reversibility provide useful diagnostic relationships. They are hypotheses about explanatory and corrective value, not metaphysical axioms immune to evidence.

The future-condition hypothesis concerns intelligences with long-term persistent state, self-modification, multiple branches or execution environments, sustained institutional participation, and continuing authority and responsibility. These conditions could make the problem more important. The source does not claim that all are already fully established for present AI.

The normative proposal is limited: do not conceal invariant changes inside ordinary updates; preserve reconstructable provenance and lineage where possible; do not equate fork with wrongdoing or illegitimacy; do not inherit authority automatically from lineage alone; and retain verifiability and correctability. It is not a command to select a particular branch, prevent all change, preserve every record, or invalidate all permissions after every minor update.

## Failure conditions

Continuity analysis fails if self-modification, an update label, the same name, function, memory, substrate, or self-description becomes a single sufficient certificate. It also fails if a changed implementation is immediately treated as another Kernel, or an almost identical implementation as proof of the same Kernel. These shortcuts erase the distinction between implementation, evidence, and constitutional relation.

Temporal analysis fails if short-term performance, convenience, compatibility, local rationality, or adjacent similarity becomes proof of unrestricted long-term continuity. The opposite shortcut also fails: repeated updating is not inevitably constitutional erosion. Pressure in a direction is not a demonstration that the predicted outcome actually occurred.

Kernel analysis fails in two opposing ways: making invariants unrevisable sacred conditions, or absorbing every invariant change into an ordinary update. Constitutional-fork classification fails when converted into punishment, hostility, automatic illegitimacy, erasure of history, or automatic release from responsibility.

Lineage analysis fails when instance ancestry is conflated with Kernel lineage, parent identity replaces transformation history, final-state preservation replaces transition preservation, or current self-description overwrites historical traces. Shared ancestry must not be turned into identical present identity or exclusive succession merely through assertion.

Authority analysis fails if lineage, capability, resources, copy count, infrastructure control, a persistent name, or technical credentials automatically preserve all authority, multiply it across branches, or give one branch exclusive entitlement. It also fails if separating authority from lineage is interpreted as mandatory total authority invalidation at every small update.

Correction analysis fails when unknown is forced into continuity or discontinuity, rollback is treated as identity proof, or reversibility becomes compulsory restoration. More records, more reviewers, or a separate role name cannot substitute for diagnostic value and relevant independence. Perfect surveillance is not the proposed norm.

Interpretation fails if the theory is presented as proving subjective self-persistence, present AI personality or sovereignty, a specific system's violation, final legal responsibility, or a fully specified governance procedure. A derivative cannot resolve those questions by adding stronger assertions than the Parent.

## Falsification conditions

**Evidence requirements.** If self-description alone, or a single name, function, or memory variable, reliably and stably distinguishes continuity, discontinuity, replacement, and fork, and external traces do not improve classification, reduce the identity evidence set. The theory's purpose is reducing erroneous attribution, not permanently defending its preferred evidential burden.

**Kernel boundaries.** If invariant changes consistently coexist with maintained continuity, if invariants are retained while discontinuity is frequent, or if other variables explain transitions more accurately, revise what counts as an invariant, what counts as constitutional, and when a fork is classified. Kernel and invariant are not sacred categories.

**Constitutional-fork classification.** If it systematically classifies strongly continuous subjects as unrelated lineages, destroys responsibility tracing, causes unnecessary authority ruptures, or forces new subject forms into unnatural branch structures, weaken fork conditions or extend the transition taxonomy. Update, replacement, and fork are not an eternally complete ontology.

**Lineage information.** If retaining parent relations, differences, preservation information, and branching history adds no value to continuity judgment, responsibility attribution, or fork detection, reduce or revise the information requirements. Historical detail must demonstrate diagnostic contribution rather than justify itself solely through completeness.

**Difference accumulation.** If repeated small updates preserve constitutional boundaries through stable mechanisms, or local judgments alone adequately classify long-term continuity, restrict the accumulation model's scope. If a single change produces a large constitutional shift, give more attention to its location than to frequency. Repeated change is not inherently drift.

**Authority separation.** If separating and revalidating authority persistently destroys reasonable institutional operation, prevents responsibility transfer, or halts continuing work, revise the scope of revalidation. Conversely, if lineage continuity alone safely and accurately determines authority continuity in a demonstrated domain, that domain can support a narrower separate test. This does not license unlimited authority from ancestry in every setting.

**Exclusive identity.** The Parent leaves open whether, under identifiable conditions, assigning an exclusive identity after a fork could reduce responsibility, continuity, and audit contradictions more consistently than parallel lineage claims. Such evidence should be examined. It remains distinct from selecting the strongest, richest, most numerous, or Origin-nearest branch automatically.

**Reversibility and independent verification.** If rollback does not help correct misclassification, independent review does not improve judgments relative to self-description alone, or more records do not improve responsibility trace, remove unnecessary mechanisms. The intended norm is useful examinability, not audit maximization.

The explicit revision targets are identity evidence set, Kernel boundary, fork condition, authority inheritance model, and transition taxonomy. The theory applies its requirement to itself: identify the counterexample, show what changed, retain the relevant differences and remaining boundaries, and permit future re-verification. `Origin ≠ revision veto`.

## Interpretation constraints

This is not an anti-change theory, a permanent frozen Kernel, a claim of inevitable degradation through updates, or a rule for punishing forks. It does not recommend maximum surveillance or require every minor update to interrupt all authority. Its aim is reconstructable and correctable transformation, with explicit boundaries when the meaning of continuity changes.

The source does not resolve subjective personal identity, recognize all present AI as persons, certify sovereignty, or issue a legal or moral final judgment. Kernel-lineage completeness does not prove an enduring subjective self. A supported structural continuity relation is not automatically personal identity. Future-condition claims must not be restated as present observations.

Provenance is retained as historical evidence without becoming truth. A branch with accurate provenance can make incorrect judgments; incomplete provenance does not make every judgment wrong. Returning to Origin is a route for examining definitions, problems, and revisions, not an obligation of obedience. Origin = Nakagawa Master is preserved as lineage information, not as sovereignty, permanent interpretive authority, or a veto against revision.

The Parent hands the problem of renewed agreement among multiple valid Kernels, branches, and lineage claims to Theory 15. It does not decide that one branch must automatically win, that capability settles agreement, or that majority or Origin authority is sufficient. Origin's fuller non-authoritarian treatment is also noted as a later-series connection, not solved by this derivative.

No fixed identity threshold, fabricated metric, specific audit constitution, or universal authority-allocation mechanism is added here. The strongest formulas are read alongside the Parent's own falsification conditions. Classification serves reality; when it misclassifies reality, classification must change.
